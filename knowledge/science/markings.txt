# Ordinary white face and leg markings (stars, blazes, socks)

## Bottom line

**There is no single “ordinary markings” gene or validated DNA test for a star, blaze, socks, or other common white markings.** They are best modeled as a **quantitative, polygenic white-marking score** influenced most strongly by variants and haplotypes at *KIT* and *MITF*, with additional effects from *MC1R* and other loci. [web:150][web:156]

The strongest published evidence comes from Haase et al. (2013), a GWAS of **1,077 Franches-Montagnes horses**. It found seven QTL that together explained **54% of genetic variance** in total white-marking score; *KIT* and *MITF* alone explained **26%**. [web:150][web:156]

## Primary paper

**Haase, B., Brooks, S. A., Schlumbaum, A., Azor, P. J., Bailey, E., Alaeddine, F., Mevissen, M., Burger, D., Poncet, P.-A., Rieder, S., & Leeb, T. (2013). “Accumulating Mutations in Series of Haplotypes at the *KIT* and *MITF* Loci Are Major Determinants of White Markings in Franches-Montagnes Horses.” *PLoS ONE* 8(9):e75071.**  
DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0075071](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0075071)  
PubMed: [24069340](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24069340/)  
Full text: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3787084/ [web:150][web:148]

The study used standardized scores for white on the head, forelimbs, and hindlimbs in **1,077 Franches-Montagnes horses**, genotyped with the Illumina 50K equine SNP chip. [web:156][web:150]

## What is established

### Markings are heritable

Ordinary white markings are clearly heritable, but the phenotype is quantitative and variable. The same broad genetic tendency can produce anything from a tiny star or pastern to a broad blaze and four stockings, while the exact shape and placement remain unpredictable. [web:159][web:150]

The Franches-Montagnes GWAS found seven loci explaining 54% of genetic variance in total white-marking score. This is strong evidence for a polygenic architecture, not a single all-or-nothing “markings gene.” [web:150][web:156]

### *KIT* is a major contributor

The strongest *KIT* signal was on **ECA3**, the chromosome containing the *KIT* gene. In the Franches-Montagnes sample, *KIT* accounted for about **10% of genetic variance in leg markings** and contributed more strongly to white markings in chestnut horses. [web:148][web:150]

This does **not** mean that every horse with socks carries a known sabino or dominant-white allele. Ordinary leg white can arise from common *KIT*-region haplotypes and other modifiers without reaching the threshold of a named white-spotting allele. [web:150][web:151]

### *MITF* is a major contributor

The strongest *MITF* signal was on **ECA16**, within intron 1B of *MITF* (\(p \leq 10^{-15}\)). In the Franches-Montagnes study, *MITF* accounted for about **23% of genetic variance in head markings** across all horses. [web:148][web:150]

*MITF* therefore contributes more strongly to facial white, while *KIT* contributes relatively more to leg white. The authors concluded that *KIT* and *MITF* act additively and that accumulating haplotypes progressively increase white-marking extent. [web:148][web:156]

### *MC1R* also matters

*MC1R*/Extension affected marking extent as well. In the Franches-Montagnes data, *MC1R* explained about **10% of variation in leg markings**; *KIT* and *MC1R* each explained about 10% of leg-marking variation. [web:148]

This means a simulator should not treat markings as independent of base color: chestnut, bay, and black-based horses can have different white-marking distributions and intensities.

## Candidate regions beyond *KIT* and *MITF*

The seven QTL in the Franches-Montagnes study included *MC1R*, *KIT*, and *MITF*, plus four additional loci. The paper does not establish causal mutations for the ordinary-markings QTL; they are association signals, not validated tests. [web:150][web:156]

A later, smaller white-marking GWAS in a different population reported candidate regions containing *ZEB2*, *KYNU*, *SLC36A1*, *STX12*, *MAP3K6*, and *GJB2*, but did **not** find the *KIT* and *MITF* signals expected from prior work. This inconsistency reinforces that ordinary markings are population- and phenotype-scoring-dependent, and that no universal set of causal variants is established. [web:160]

## What is not established

- **No causal mutation** has been proven for ordinary stars, blazes, or socks outside known white-spotting alleles.
- **No validated commercial test** predicts ordinary marking extent or placement.
- **No single locus** explains the full phenotype.
- **No exact marking map**—for example, “this genotype makes a left hind sock”—is possible from current science.
- **No universal allele frequency** can be assigned, because effect sizes and haplotype frequencies differ among breeds and studies. [web:150][web:159][web:160]

## Distinguish from known white-spotting alleles

| Pattern | Established genetic status | Relationship to ordinary markings |
|---|---|---|
| Sabino 1 | *KIT* partial loss of exon 17; autosomal incompletely dominant | Often gives two or more white legs, extensive blaze, and ticking/roaning; a named allele, not ordinary-markings genetics. [web:157] |
| Splashed white | Multiple *MITF* and *PAX3* variants; dominant with variable expression | Broad blaze and extended white markings; distinct from ordinary marking score. [web:155] |
| Dominant white | Multiple *KIT* variants | Can range from extensive face/leg white to near-complete white; distinct from ordinary markings. [web:158] |
| Ordinary markings | Polygenic; *KIT*, *MITF*, *MC1R*, and other loci contribute | Stars, strips, blazes, socks, stockings, and variable white extent without a known causal allele. [web:150][web:156] |

## Simulator implementation

Use a **continuous white-marking score**, then generate the actual marks procedurally.

```text
white_marking_score = 0.0–1.0

white_marking_score =
    KIT_marking_component
  + MITF_marking_component
  + MC1R_base_color_effect
  + other_polygenic_components
  + stochastic_developmental_noise
```

Recommended allele model:

```text
KIT_MARKING: additive 0–2 dose
MITF_MARKING: additive 0–2 dose
MC1R: E/E, E/e, e/e affects both base color and marking expression
```

Then split the score into head and leg components:

```text
head_white = MITF_weighted_score + noise
leg_white  = KIT_weighted_score + MC1R_effect + noise
```

Practical visual rules:

- Low score: small star, snip, coronet, or one low pastern.
- Medium score: stripe or blaze, one or two socks, uneven leg white.
- High score: broad blaze, four stockings, belly spots, or ticking—but only if you also model sabino-like expression.
- Use left/right and fore/hind asymmetry; real markings are not bilaterally symmetrical.
- Do not force a blaze and four socks to be inherited as one indivisible unit.
- Do not let ordinary markings produce body patches, glass eyes, or near-white coats unless a separate white-spotting allele is present.

## Inheritance model

The best-supported implementation is **additive polygenic inheritance**:

- More white-increasing *KIT* and *MITF* haplotypes produce more white.
- *MC1R* modifies the expression of the score.
- Developmental noise determines the precise shape and side of each marking.
- Known white-spotting alleles should be separate overlays, not part of the ordinary-markings score. [web:150][web:156][web:157]

A simple, defensible breeding rule is:

```text
foal_marking_score =
    0.35 * sire_score
  + 0.35 * dam_score
  + KIT_MITF_additive_effect
  + noise
```

Do not present this as a scientifically exact prediction; it is a simulation abstraction consistent with the published polygenic evidence. [web:159][web:150]

## Breeds and frequencies

The best-powered published dataset is Franches-Montagnes: **1,077 horses**, phenotyped and genotyped in the 2013 GWAS. Its percentages are genetic-variance estimates within that breed and scoring system, not universal allele frequencies. [web:150][web:156]

A Spanish-horse study of 70 Menorca Purebreds and 70 Spanish Purebreds found that the *KIT* p.Arg682His variant was associated with facial and total white markings in Menorca Purebreds, with estimated relative contributions of 47.6% for head and 43.4% for total score in that breed. These are breed-specific estimates, not global frequencies. [web:154]

## Effects outside coat color

There is no evidence that ordinary white markings affect height, speed, muscle, bone structure, hoof shape, feathering, or mane/tail length. Their established phenotype is limited to white hair in the face and lower limbs, sometimes extending to body white in more extreme spotting phenotypes. [web:150][web:159]

## Future science

A definitive ordinary-markings study would need:

1. Large, multi-breed cohorts with standardized photographic scoring.
2. Separate models for head white, forelimb white, and hindlimb white.
3. Whole-genome sequencing rather than SNP-chip-only GWAS.
4. Replication across breeds.
5. Functional validation of *KIT* and *MITF* regulatory variants.
6. Explicit exclusion or modeling of known sabino, splashed white, dominant white, tobiano, and gray phenotypes. [web:150][web:160]

EARLIER NOTE (2026-10-12, before round four)
--------------------------------------------

At a glance
-----------

Trait | Ordinary white face and leg markings - star, stripe, snip, blaze, coronet, sock, stocking
Genetics | Polygenic: many small-effect loci, with no single causal variant
Also | Substantial non-genetic developmental variation in how far pigment cells migrated
Testable | No commercial test, because there is no one locus to test
Health | None. White hair is not a disorder; the deafness risk belongs to the splash alleles specifically

Why this is its own thing and not splash
----------------------------------------

The single most useful finding here is a negative one. The study that identified SW1 deliberately assembled 112 horses with minimal white markings - the population you would expect a minimally-expressed splash allele to be hiding in - and found no splash allele at all. Ordinary stars and socks are simply not splash.

They are also not one gene. Marking extent behaves like a quantitative trait: continuously distributed, moderately heritable, and responsive to selection, which is the signature of many loci of small effect rather than one locus with big ones. Breeders have selected both for and against white for centuries and moved the population gradually in both directions, which is exactly what you cannot do with a single dominant.

On top of the genetics sits genuine developmental noise. Pigment cells migrate from the neural crest during gestation, and how far they get before the coat is fixed is not fully determined by the genome - which is why full siblings, and even clones, differ in their markings, and why a marking is usable as an individual identifier on a registration diagram at all.

The confusion this locus reproduces on purpose
----------------------------------------------

At its top end this gene gives a broad blaze and high stockings, which is very hard to tell from a minimally expressed sabino or splash by eye. That ambiguity is not a shortcoming of the model - it is the thing the literature keeps saying out loud, and the reason testing exists. A horse with four white legs and a bald face may be a heavily marked ordinary horse or a minimally marked pattern carrier, and only its genotype settles it.
