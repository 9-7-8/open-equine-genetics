ROUND THREE (the owner's answer to question D3, 2026-10-15)
------------------------------------------------------------
Asked which allele is ancestral at PDK4 g.38973231A>G and CKM g.15884567G>A. No address
has been opened by a maintainer. Her answer, condensed, with every point and address
she gave:

  - Best-supported inference: PDK4 A is ancestral, CKM G is ancestral. PDK4 G and CKM A
    are the derived, non-reference alleles, but both are very old in horses, not recent
    Thoroughbred inventions. (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4284583/)
  - PDK4 g.38973231A>G: ECA4, intron 2, BIEC2-903605. A is the reference allele and the
    one associated with elite racing. Pre-domestication horses in a 2014 ancient-DNA
    study carried the reference allele at most loci assayed, and PDK4 was not among the
    exceptions. (https://patents.google.com/patent/AU2009290452B2/en ; PMC4284583)
  - CKM g.15884567G>A: ECA9, BIEC2-109575. G is the reference allele, A the
    alternative. The Late Pleistocene stallion CGG10023 (about 16,099 calibrated years
    before present) carried the alternative A allele, so A is at least that old; the
    authors called such variants standing variation, not new mutations from
    domestication or racing.
  - "Ancestral" here is inferred from the reference allele, the SNP's orientation and
    the ancient genomes, not from a formal reconstruction; the 2014 study used the donkey
    as an outgroup but prints no per-SNP ancestral table for these two.
  - In the association work, A/A and A/G at PDK4 were commoner among elite Thoroughbreds
    than G/G (a dominant model); the CKM effect was weaker. Treat both as performance
    markers, not speed genes.

The session's reading: the reference genome comes from one Thoroughbred, so a reference
allele is not ancestral by that fact alone. For PDK4 there is more: the ancient horses
carried A. For CKM the only evidence is that G is the reference; the ancient stallion
shows A is old, not that G came first.

What the gene files do with it: pdk4's wild-type flag moves from G to A. ckm already
flagged its reference allele (G of the papers) and is unchanged. The answer puts CKM on
chromosome 9; the gene file says 10 (from an earlier answer). Not changed; asked again.

LATEST FINDINGS (the owner's second research answer, 2026-10-12)
-----------------------------------------------------------------
The first answer, kept below, looked for CKM as a coat-colour gene and found none. The
owner asked again about the racing claim. No address here has been opened by a maintainer.
- CKM is a real gene on chromosome 10, highly expressed in skeletal and heart muscle
  (NCBI Gene 100065641).
- The racing claim is Gu, MacHugh, McGivney, Park, Katz and Hill 2010, Equine Veterinary
  Journal 42(s38):569-575 (the second answer called it "Hill et al."; Hill is the last
  author). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4097168/
- The variant: g.15884567G>A, at ECA10:15,884,567 in EquCab2.0, SNP BIEC2-109575, in
  intron 4 - not a coding change. The A allele was commoner in elite Thoroughbreds than
  in non-winners (allelic test P = 0.02066; odds ratio given as 0.4089, which is stated
  for the G allele). A computer analysis suggested G disrupts a binding motif that A
  keeps; that is a hypothesis, not shown. A patent built on the work proposed a
  several-gene model with PDK4 and COX4I2; a patent claim is not validation.
- The study found new SNPs in ACTN3, CKM and COX4I2; three SNPs in
  CKM and COX4I2 differed between elite Thoroughbreds and horses that had never won.
  In a further independent 130 horses the CKM associations did NOT validate; only
  COX4I2 held in the combined analysis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21059062/
- A later Quarter Horse study of the same variant found it polymorphic and not associated with racing performance or line.
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0737080615005699
  (That answer wrote the variant "CKM g.22999655C>A". That is a slip: chr23:g.22,999,655C>A
  is the DMRT3 gait variant, as the owner's DMRT3 research shows. The CKM variant is
  g.15884567G>A throughout.)
- CK-M expression in blood rises after 30 minutes of exercise in Thoroughbreds; in
  muscle it did not change significantly. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647075/
- Verdict: a candidate that failed to replicate. Not a validated marker; not to be
  generalised to other breeds; far weaker than MSTN. No coat effect.
- The research suggests a simulator could treat it as a weak, noisy modifier. This data
  records only what is established, which is nothing: the gene file states no effect.

CKM is not an established horse coat-color locus. In genetics, CKM usually refers to creatine kinase M-type (also called CKMM), a muscle-energy enzyme gene—not a gene known to control equine red/black pigment, dilution, patterning, height, speed, hoof shape, feathering, or mane/tail color. [edrn.cancer](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/biomarkers/ckm/)

What CKM actually is
--------------------

- CKM / CKMM encodes the muscle-type subunit of creatine kinase, an enzyme that helps regenerate ATP from creatine phosphate in tissues with high, fluctuating energy demands, especially skeletal and cardiac muscle. [edrn.cancer](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/biomarkers/ckm/)
- In medicine, CKM is also used as shorthand for cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, a human-health framework linking heart disease, kidney disease, obesity, and type 2 diabetes; this is unrelated to horse coat genetics. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39705194/)

Why it is not a horse color gene
--------------------------------

UC Davis’s equine coat-color resources identify the core red/black-pigment system as MC1R (Extension / E locus) and ASIP (Agouti / A locus):

Locus | Gene | Established alleles | Effect
Extension | MC1R | E, e, rare ea | E permits black pigment; e/e produces chestnut/red-based coats. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)
Agouti | ASIP | A, a | A restricts black to points, producing bay on an E-based horse; a/a allows uniform black. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

There is no UC Davis-listed CKM allele, phenotype, or coat-color test analogous to E, A, Cr, Dun, Champagne, Silver, Gray, or the white-pattern loci. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

Possible confusion
------------------

You may be thinking of one of these:

- CKM as creatine kinase M-type — relevant to muscle energy metabolism, but not a validated equine coat-color or conformation marker in mainstream horse genetics. [edrn.cancer](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/biomarkers/ckm/)
- CKM syndrome — a human cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health concept, not an equine trait. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic-syndrome)
- A similarly abbreviated horse locus, such as KIT, MITF, PAX3, MSTN, DMRT3, or LCORL — these do have established or actively researched equine associations with white patterning, gait, muscle type, and height/body size.

Bottom line for a simulator
---------------------------

For an equine-genetics simulation, I would not model CKM as a coat-color, pigment, height, speed, muscle, bone, hoof, feathering, or mane/tail-color locus. The evidence-based base-pigment model should use:

- MC1R: E/_ = black pigment possible; e/e = chestnut/red base. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)
- ASIP: A/_ = bay distribution of black; a/a = black distribution when E is present. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

If you meant a different abbreviation—such as CKM from a specific breed registry, paper, or another spelling—send the context and I can map it to the correct equine locus.
