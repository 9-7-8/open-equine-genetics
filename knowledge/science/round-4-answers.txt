Sunshine: which frequency claim is right?
  Both statements describe different things; the population-sequencing result is the one
  that addresses allele frequency.
  - Holl et al. 2019 identified SLC45A2 c.568G>A (p.Gly190Arg), proposed as Csun, in a
    small phenotyped dataset of about 130 horses. No horse was homozygous; it was proposed
    to cause a pearl-like recessive dilution in compound heterozygotes. It did not
    establish that sunshine is rare across breeds.
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31006892/ ; https://doi.org/10.1111/age.12790
  - Durward-Akhurst et al. 2024 sequenced 605 horses from 48 breeds: 146 heterozygotes and
    390 homozygotes for the variant. The non-reference A allele was the major allele in
    all 12 breed groups examined, above 0.86 in Arabian, Belgian, Morgan, Thoroughbred and
    Quarter Horse. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006912/ ;
    https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57872-8
  Correct interpretation: c.568G>A is not rare population-wide. Its status as the causal
  sunshine dilution is uncertain; Durward-Akhurst et al. say more work is needed. Either
  exclude Csun until validated, or include it as putative, with no dilution by default.
  "Very rare, in one lineage" is not supported as a population-frequency claim; it may
  reflect the discovery context.

Tiger eye: TE1 and TE2 in Puerto Rican Paso Fino
  Primary paper: Mack et al. 2017, "Two variants in SLC24A5 are associated with

  Established: TE1 and TE2 are associated with tiger-eye iris pigmentation in PRPF; most

  heterozygous. Modelling advice: two alleles of one SLC24A5 eye locus; no effect on body,

# Cervical vertebral malformation (wobbler syndrome) and megaesophagus

## Bottom line

**Cervical vertebral compressive myelopathy (CVCM), commonly called wobbler syndrome, has a likely genetic component but no identified causal mutation or validated DNA test.** It is best modeled as a **multifactorial, polygenic developmental disease** modified by sex, rapid growth, diet, exercise, trauma, and conformation. [web:198][web:195][web:203]

**Megaesophagus has a strong breed- and family-level case for heritability in Friesian horses, but no causal gene or validated DNA test has been published.** It should be modeled as a **heritable, probably complex trait in Friesians**, with environmental and acquired causes also possible. [web:197][web:199][web:214]

## Cervical vertebral malformation / wobbler syndrome

### What it is

CVCM is compression of the cervical spinal cord caused by malformation, instability, or stenosis of the neck vertebrae. It produces ataxia—“wobbler syndrome”—usually most obvious in the hind limbs, and may be dynamic or static depending on the site and type of compression. [web:198][web:203]

The older umbrella term “cervical vertebral malformation” includes several developmental abnormalities. A related but distinct condition, **equine complex vertebral malformation (ECVM)**, involves malformations at the cervicothoracic junction, especially C6/C7, and is not the same as a proven CVCM diagnosis. [web:200][web:207]

### Genetic evidence

UC Davis states that a genetic component of CVCM is likely but remains elusive; no causative mutation has been identified. It also advises that affected horses should not be bred because the condition is potentially heritable. [web:198]

The evidence is consistent with **polygenic susceptibility**, not a single Mendelian allele:

- Thoroughbreds, Quarter Horses, and Warmbloods are reported to have genetic predisposition. [web:203]
- Males are more commonly affected than females. [web:203][web:195]
- Type I CVCM in young horses is multifactorial, involving inheritance, diet, rapid growth, trauma, and growth rate. [web:195]
- Some studies failed to demonstrate simple genetic determination, while others support a genetic influence on neck length, vertebral biomechanics, body size, and cartilage-growth response. [web:195][web:205]
- No validated gene, locus, or DNA test is available. [web:198]

### Best inheritance model

Use a **liability-threshold polygenic model**:

```text
CVCM_liability =
    polygenic_susceptibility
  + sex_effect
  + rapid_growth_effect
  + high_energy_diet_effect
  + trauma_or_exercise_effect
  + random_developmental_noise

if CVCM_liability > threshold:
    wobbler_syndrome = true
```

Do **not** implement a simple dominant or recessive “wobbler gene.” UC Davis specifically notes that no causative mutation has been identified despite ongoing research. [web:198]

### Simulator rules

- Make CVCM a health trait, not a coat-color locus.
- Give it low baseline incidence and a continuous liability score.
- Increase risk for rapid growth, high-energy diets, male sex, and certain conformational or breed backgrounds.
- Allow affected horses to be bred in-game only if the simulation deliberately models irresponsible breeding; otherwise apply a breeding-restriction rule.
- Do not assign a genomic coordinate, allele frequency, or carrier status, because none is validated.

## Megaesophagus

### What it is

Megaesophagus is abnormal esophageal function with chronic dilation and impaired transport of feed to the stomach. In horses it can cause recurrent choke, regurgitation/nasal reflux, weight loss, aspiration pneumonia, and death. [web:197][web:214]

### Evidence for heritability

The evidence is strongest in **Friesian horses**:

- A necropsy study of 852 horses over six years found a higher prevalence of severe esophageal disease, especially megaesophagus, in Friesians than in other breeds; 5 of 6 affected Friesians had marked caudal esophageal smooth-muscle hypertrophy. [web:197]
- A later morphological study reported an approximate **2% prevalence among Friesians presented to two Dutch referral clinics**, compared with **0.5% in horses of other breeds**. These are **prevalence/share of affected horses**, not allele or carrier frequencies. [web:202][web:213]
- Abnormal clumped and disorganized collagen was found in affected Friesians, including very young foals, supporting a congenital/hereditary component. [web:202][web:199]
- A 2002–2007 Dutch clinical series recorded 45 megaesophagus cases, of which 41 were Friesians, with familial clustering reported. [web:204]
- The Fenway Foundation/FHANA research program treats Friesian megaesophagus as likely complex, potentially involving connective-tissue and esophageal-function abnormalities. [web:214][web:208]

### What is not established

- **No causal mutation has been published.**
- **No validated DNA test exists.**
- **No inheritance mode is proven.** A recessive model is a plausible research hypothesis, but it has not been established.
- **No breed-general allele or carrier frequency can be cited.** The 2% and 0.5% figures are clinical prevalence estimates in specific referral populations, not genotype frequencies. [web:202][web:213]
- Ongoing family-based sequencing had narrowed candidates to 969 variants across 135 genes as of the 2021 FHANA update, with one insertion of interest, but this was not a published causal discovery. [web:208]

### Best inheritance model

For Friesians, use a **heritable complex-disease model**:

```text
megaesophagus_liability =
    Friesian_breed_background_effect
  + polygenic_connective_tissue_effect
  + familial_risk_effect
  + acquired_esophageal_disease_effect
  + random_developmental_noise

if megaesophagus_liability > threshold:
    megaesophagus = true
```

If you want a simple Mendelian abstraction, use a **recessive major-risk allele restricted to Friesian founder lines**, but label it a simulation convention. Do not present it as a known causal allele.

### Simulator rules

- Restrict elevated congenital risk primarily to Friesian ancestry.
- Make congenital/young-onset cases distinct from acquired megaesophagus in older horses.
- Include choke risk, poor weight gain, aspiration-pneumonia risk, and possible early death as consequences.
- Do not let coat-color genetics or conformation traits directly cause megaesophagus.
- Do not claim a DNA test or carrier status.

## How the two conditions differ

| Feature | CVCM / wobbler syndrome | Megaesophagus |
|---|---|---|
| Main system | Neurologic / skeletal | Digestive |
| Genetic status | Likely genetic component; no mutation found | Strong heritability evidence in Friesians; no mutation found |
| Best model | Polygenic liability plus environment | Heritable complex disease, especially in Friesians |
