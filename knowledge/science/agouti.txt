Agouti - science notes
(Copied 2026-10-12 from the Science tab of the Procedural Horse Coats wiki page for this gene.
Written for that project: it may mention how a game models the gene. Not yet audited.)

Genetic identity
----------------

Gene | ASIP - agouti signalling protein
Established variant | An 11-base-pair deletion in exon 2, causing a frameshift and loss of normal ASIP function
Alleles | A functional; a the deletion
Inheritance | Autosomal; a recessive
Epistasis | Visible only when Extension permits eumelanin. Hidden entirely on a red-based horse

Agouti's firmly established job is not deciding whether a horse can make black pigment - that is chiefly MC1R - but controlling where eumelanin is deposited in a horse that can make it. UC Davis VGL (https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

What ASIP actually does
-----------------------

MC1R sits on the melanocyte and, when stimulated, promotes eumelanin production. The ASIP protein counteracts that signalling: in the appropriate body regions and hair-growth stages it reduces MC1R-driven black pigment, letting the coat grow red or brown there while black survives in the mane, tail, legs, ear margins and other point-associated areas.

"ASIP turns black off on the body" is a useful shorthand, but the real biology is more spatial and more time-dependent than an on/off switch. The practical result is the part that holds: functional ASIP on a black-pigment-capable horse gives a bay-pattern base; no functional ASIP gives a black-pattern base. Both describe pigment distribution before dilution, grey, white spotting, roan, leopard complex, seasonal fading and sun bleaching - so a black-based horse can perfectly well look smoky, brownish, grey, white-patterned or silver-diluted.

Two alleles, and what the test really says
------------------------------------------

Genotype | Test result | On an E_ horse
A/A | Deletion not detected on either allele | Bay-pattern base
A/a | Deletion detected once | Bay-pattern base - one functional copy suffices
a/a | Deletion detected twice | Black-pattern base

Note the wording in that middle column. The standard test is a test for one 11-bp deletion, so A/A means "the deletion was not found", not "every sequence element across ASIP and its regulatory landscape is identical". It is a major-base-colour test, well validated for what it asks, and it is not a shade test.

On a red-based e/e horse the whole locus is invisible: a chestnut may be A/A, A/a or a/a and will look red regardless, because there is no coat eumelanin for ASIP to distribute. The genotype is still inherited, and still matters the moment the horse is bred to a non-chestnut.

Why four outcomes and not one
-----------------------------

Blood bay, bay, liver bay and seal brown are phenotype descriptions, not loci. Every one of them tests as the same foundation - one functional MC1R copy and one functional ASIP copy, E_A_ - and no validated equine allele separates them. What separates them in a real horse is a regulatory region, a couple of dosage effects and a polygenic tail, and what separates them here is the same list, summed into one shade score.

So agouti declares four bay Expressions and picks between them in expressionIn, by reading the rest of the genotype. Its pair-only expressionOf answers bay for any A horse and admits it is coarse - the same position the leopard complex is in, and the reason both genes list their combinations in the preview window rather than their outcomes.

There is still no seal allele - and no At
A seal brown is ShD/ShD plus a dosage term, not an allele of this gene. The A+ / A / At / a series people quote for seal brown is real in other mammals and unvalidated in horses; the shade page has the argument and the alternative designs that were rejected.

A population where dark bay is the majority class
-------------------------------------------------

The argument above is a design call, and there is one dataset that bears on it directly. A study of 313 Bosnian Mountain Horses (Cotman et al.), genotyping ASIP and DUN against the breeders' association records, reports dark bay and black as the two most representative colours of the breed - dark bay about 44%, black about 36%, ordinary bay only about 11%. So a real, closed, dark-selected population splits mostly into dark bay and black, with bright bay the minority.

That is a population this gene has to be able to produce, and it can: the black comes from a/a and the dark-versus-bright split from the shade score, which is exactly the division of labour the note above describes. The breed's file (Domestic Mountain Pony) is written that way - an a-heavy agouti pool for the black, and a deliberately ShD-skewed shade pool for the dark bay, rather than an At allele that does not exist. Whether it reads as dark bay on a horse in the world is unverified, and it is the best available test of the no-At decision, because here the target proportions are measured rather than guessed - the Verification tab carries it.

The same study is a caution about phenotype data generally: it reports genotype/phenotype discrepancies, with horses recorded as one colour class genotyping as another. Registry colour fields are descriptions written by people, and dark bay against black against brown is exactly where that goes wrong.

Why A+ and At were proposed, and why they failed
------------------------------------------------

Older colour literature taught an allelic series - A+ > A > At > a - with A+ as wild bay, restricting black still further; A as ordinary bay; and At as seal brown, allowing more black but keeping tan around the muzzle, eyes, flanks, elbow and stifle. It was a reasonable historical hypothesis, built on phenotype, breeding observation and analogy to agouti systems in mammals where multiple ASIP alleles genuinely do exist. But a useful phenotype classification is not proof of an allelic series at a particular gene.

Wild bay is a real-looking category - light red body, black tightly confined to mane, tail, ear edges and the lower leg, sometimes stopping near the fetlock. No reproducible causative ASIP variant has been established that segregates with it, is distinct from ordinary functional A, survives controlled family or association data, and has functional evidence behind it. Horses called wild bay test plainly A/A or A/a. The appearance is not imaginary; the allele is unevidenced, and the look is adequately explained by polygenic pigment variation, regulatory variants, pangaé-type ventral lightening, dun background, season, age, nutrition, lighting and subjective labelling.

Seal brown is the harder case, because the phenotype overlaps sun-faded black, very dark bay, black with mealy expression, dark bay under shade polygenes, silver on black, and early grey or roan. The central problem is genotype-phenotype mismatch: seal brown horses routinely test as ordinary agouti-positive animals rather than as a reproducible third genotype. There was also a commercial "brown" test marketed from a marker near the agouti region; it was not accepted as causal after inconsistent results, including results in Miniature Horses that did not match expected inheritance. Equine Tapestry (https://www.equinetapestry.blog/base-color-variation-mealy-and-seal-brown/) A linked marker can track a look in one selected population without being the causal variant, and it fails when recombination or a different background breaks the association. A patented or marketed test is not automatically a validated allele test.

The precise statement is worth getting right, because it is not "these alleles have been disproved". Science rarely proves a negative that broadly. It is: no distinct equine A+ or At has been molecularly demonstrated, the classic four-allele series is unsupported, and neither should be used as a confirmed genotype in a breeding prediction or a simulation. The seal-brown phenotype may well have a genetic basis - ASIP regulation, another locus, or several modifiers - but finding it would be a new discovery, not a vindication of the textbook At.

Genotype does not always predict phenotype
------------------------------------------

The Bosnian study above is not alone in reporting this. A 709-horse genotype-phenotype dataset found horses classified as brown, dark bay, bay and even white despite being homozygous for the known ASIP deletion, and concluded that additional genes are involved. PMC6447268 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6447268/) That does not overturn the deletion's established effect - it reinforces two things this gene is built around: visible phenotype can be masked or modified by other loci, and colour classification by people is itself noisy.

What is settled, and what is not
--------------------------------

Claim | Status
ASIP is the equine agouti gene and distributes eumelanin with MC1R | Established
The 11-bp exon-2 deletion is a loss-of-function non-agouti allele | Established
Agouti is visually hidden on e/e chestnuts and still inherited | Established
Standard testing distinguishes A/A, A/a, a/a | Established for the known deletion - and for nothing else
Wild bay is a separate A+ allele | Unvalidated. A recognisable phenotype, not a confirmed molecular allele
Seal brown is a separate At allele | Unvalidated and presently unsupported
Bay, dark bay, brown and black can be diagnosed from MC1R and ASIP alone | False
A+ and At can never be found by future work | Not a scientific conclusion. They are not validated now; sequencing could reveal new variants or mechanisms

Where the mod departs
---------------------

On this locus, mostly it does not - and that was not luck
The source's own recommendation for a simulator is: model ASIP with only A and a; use E_A_ for bay and E_a/a for black; and make wild bay, dark bay, seal-brown-like expression, countershading and the degree of black extension modifier-driven phenotype parameters rather than Mendelian alleles, kept probabilistic and allowed to differ by breed. That is a description of what this gene and shade already do between them, down to the breed-linked frequency skew in the breed files. The call to drop A+ and At was made before this source arrived and is confirmed by it.

Two real simplifications remain. Dosage is not modelled at this locus: the mod, like the literature, treats A/A and A/a as the same bay-pattern base, so a homozygote is not a brighter bay - where dark-versus-bright lives is the shade score, which reads the whole genotype rather than the agouti pair. And the four bay outcomes are named here where a laboratory would decline to name them; that is a deliberate gameplay choice, since a locus that reports only "bay" would hide the variation the shade system exists to create.
