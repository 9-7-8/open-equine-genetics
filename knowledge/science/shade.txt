# Bay shade (light bay to dark bay)

## Bottom line

**Yes—bay shade now has a published associated locus, called *Shade*, on horse chromosome 22 near *ASIP* and *RALY*.** Corbin et al. (2020) mapped variation in the amount/distribution of black pigment in bay horses to an approximately 0.5 Mb region on ECA22; the lead SNP lies between *ASIP* and *RALY*. However, **no causal mutation has been proven and no validated commercial DNA test exists**. [page:0]

It is best modeled as an **additive, quantitative modifier of bay phenotype**, not as a simple dominant or recessive “light bay/dark bay” gene. The effect is on the amount and extent of black eumelanin in the body coat; it does not create bay from a chestnut, nor does it override the base-color system. [page:0]

## Primary paper

**Corbin, L. J., Pope, J., Sanson, J., Antczak, D. F., Miller, D., Sadeghi, R., & Brooks, S. A. (2020). “An Independent Locus Upstream of *ASIP* Controls Variation in the Shade of the Bay Coat Colour in Horses.” *Genes* 11(6):606.**  
DOI: [10.3390/genes11060606](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes11060606)  
PubMed: [32486210](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32486210/)  
Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7349280/ [page:0]

The study used **126 bay horses** after quality control—39 Quarter Horses, 61 Arabians, 23 Persian Horses, 2 Standardbreds, and 1 Thoroughbred—from owner-submitted photographs and existing genotype/sequence datasets. Horses were ranked from light bay, with black limited to points, to very dark bay, with black extending across much of the body. [page:0]

## What the locus does

Bay is produced when a horse has at least one functional *MC1R* \(E\) allele and at least one dominant *ASIP* \(A\) allele: black pigment is restricted largely to the mane, tail, lower legs, ear edges, and related “points,” while the body remains red/bay. [page:0][web:77]

The *Shade* locus modifies **how much black pigment appears in the body area** of a bay horse:

- Lower *Shade* rank: lighter bay, with black largely confined to the points.
- Higher *Shade* rank: darker bay, with more black eumelanin distributed through the body.
- The extreme end approaches black-like body pigmentation while retaining enough lighter flank/nose areas to distinguish it from \(a/a\) black. [page:0]

This is therefore a **quantitative bay-shade modifier**, not a separate base-color allele.

## Published genomic evidence

### Associated region

The GWAS identified a single significant region spanning about **0.5 Mb on ECA22**, immediately upstream of *ASIP*. The broader candidate region examined was **ECA22:24,841,938–25,178,735** and contained 21 annotated genes. [page:0]

The lead GWAS SNP was:

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| SNP | AX-103117105 |
| Assembly | **EquCab2.0** |
| Position | **ECA22:24,998,294** |
| Effect allele | T |
| Effect-allele frequency | 0.38 in the study sample |
| Effect | −32.2 shade-rank units per T allele; approximately one-quarter of the 126-horse ranking range |
| \(p\)-value | \(9.76 \times 10^{-15}\) |
| Location | Between *ASIP* and *RALY*—downstream of *ASIP*, upstream of *RALY* |

The authors explicitly state that the lead SNP is **unlikely to be causal** and is probably tagging functional variation elsewhere in the high-LD region. [page:0]

A second approximately independent index SNP, AX-103538677, was also associated (\(p = 1.22 \times 10^{-7}\); effect allele C, frequency 0.35). No additional genome-wide significant signal remained after conditioning on these two SNPs. [page:0]

### Candidate causal variant

The most plausible functional candidate is a variable **(GGCAGC)\(_n\) repeat in exon 6 of *RALY***, producing a serine/glycine motif change:

- Reference allele: reference sequence.
- 6 bp deletion: **NC_009165.2:g.24,917,006_24,917,007delGGCAGC**.
- 6 bp insertion: **NC_009165.2:g.24,917,006_24,917,007insGGCAGC**.
- Protein consequence: ENSECAP00000010319.1:p.Ser238_Gly239insGS or delGS.
- Association with bay-shade rank: \(p = 3.802 \times 10^{-10}\).

All coordinates here are **EquCab2.0**, because the authors found an apparent artificial duplication around ECA22:25.7–25.87 Mb in EquCab3.0 and used EquCab2.0 for reporting. [page:0]

The *RALY* indel is associated, but the GWAS lead SNP remained the better predictor in the available data. Because the region has strong linkage disequilibrium, the authors could **not distinguish** whether the causal change is in *RALY*, in *ASIP* regulation, or in another linked regulatory element. [page:0]

## Inheritance

The published evidence supports an **additive allele-dosage effect**, not a clean dominant/recessive model:

- Each additional T allele at AX-103117105 reduced the bay-shade rank by about 32 units, i.e. moved the horse toward lighter bay. [page:0]
- The effect was significant within Arabians (\(n=61\)), Persian Horses (\(n=23\)), and Quarter Horses (\(n=39\)), as well as in the combined sample (\(n=126\)). [page:0]
- *MC1R* also contributed to shade: \(E/E\) horses were on average darker than \(E/e\) horses. The combined *Shade* and *MC1R* effects appeared additive. [page:0]
- *ASIP* \(A/a\) versus \(A/A\) had only a weak, non-significant shade effect after accounting for the *Shade* and *MC1R* effects. [page:0]

So a practical simulator rule is:

```text
bay_shade = base_value
          + Shade_allele_dosage_effect
          + MC1R_dosage_effect
          + polygenic/environmental residual
```

Do **not** implement bay shade as a simple recessive trait unless you deliberately choose a simplified game convention.

## What is established versus unresolved

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| Is bay shade genetically influenced? | Yes; a significant ECA22 association was published in 2020. [page:0] |
| Is there a named locus? | Yes: **Shade**, near *ASIP*/*RALY* on ECA22. [page:0] |
| Is the causal variant known? | No; the lead SNP is a tag, and the *RALY* indel is a candidate but unproven. [page:0] |
| Is there a DNA test? | No validated commercial test is established from this paper. [page:0] |
| Is inheritance dominant or recessive? | Neither is established; the data fit an additive dosage model. [page:0] |
| Does it affect chestnuts? | The published study excluded \(e/e\) chestnuts; it concerns bay horses with functional black-pigment production. [page:0] |
| Does it affect black horses? | The study excluded \(a/a\) black horses; its direct evidence is for bay horses. [page:0] |

## Simulator implementation

Use a **quantitative Shade locus** with an additive allele effect:

```text
SHD/SHD = strongest lightening / least body eumelanin
SHD/shd = intermediate
shd/shd = darkest bay tendency
```

Important: the published T effect allele was associated with a **lower rank**, meaning less body black and therefore a **lighter** bay. If your simulator uses a “darkness” scale, invert the sign.

Recommended model:

```text
bay_darkness = 0.0–1.0

bay_darkness =
    0.55
  - 0.18 * count_of_T_at_Shade
  - 0.08 * MC1R_e_dosage
  + polygenic_residual
```

Then convert `bay_darkness` into visual parameters:

- Red-channel body base color.
- Amount of black ticking or shading in the body.
- Size/intensity of black points.
- Gradual transition from bright red bay through standard bay to dark/mahogany bay.

Do not use *Shade* to produce:

- Chestnut (\(e/e\)).
- True black (\(a/a\) at *ASIP*).
- Bay dun, buckskin, or other dilution phenotypes.
- Gray progression.

Those require their own established loci.

## Other shade influences

Bay shade is not wholly explained by *Shade*. The same GWAS found that *MC1R* genotype affects shade: horses with two \(E\) alleles tended to be darker than \(E/e\) horses. [page:0]

An earlier colorimetric study by Druml et al. (2018), *Czech Journal of Animal Science* 63:347–355, also found that *ASIP*/*MC1R* genotype combinations affected measured brightness and chromaticity; \(A/a\ E/E\) horses tended to be darker and were over-represented among seal brown and dark mahogany bay classifications. DOI: [10.17221/67/2018-CJAS](http://cjas.agriculturejournals.cz/artkey/cjs-201809-0002_discriminant-analysis-of-colour-measurements-reveals-allele-dosage-effect-of-asip-mc1r-in-bay-horses.php). [web:76]

Therefore, a realistic simulator should retain residual variation beyond *Shade*: nutrition/season, sun exposure, age, photographic conditions, and unmeasured polygenic factors. The Corbin study itself used owner photographs with variable lighting and noted that future work should use spectrophotometry or color-calibrated images. [page:0]

## Effects outside coat color

No published evidence shows that the bay-shade *Shade* locus affects height, speed, muscle, bone structure, hoof shape, feathering, or mane/tail length. Its demonstrated phenotype is pigmentation: the extent of black eumelanin in the bay body coat. [page:0]

The authors note a theoretical possibility of pleiotropy because *ASIP* regulatory variation can affect obesity-related traits in mice, but this is **speculative for the equine Shade allele** and must not be implemented as an established health or conformation effect. [page:0]

## Breeds and allele frequency

The published association was demonstrated in a mixed sample of **126 horses**: Quarter Horse, Arabian, Persian Horse, Standardbred, and Thoroughbred. The T effect allele at AX-103117105 had a frequency of **0.38** in that sample; this is an **allele frequency**, not a share of carriers, and it comes from the 2020 Corbin et al. GWAS. [page:0]

That frequency should not be generalized to breeds or worldwide horse populations without breed-specific genotyping. A later Konik study found that the tested *RALY* polymorphism was in complete linkage disequilibrium with the 11-bp *ASIP* indel in 73 Polish Primitive Horses, with all tested bay or bay-dun horses carrying the “darkening” *RALY* allele; this is breed-specific evidence about LD and does not establish a universal bay-shade allele frequency. [web:71]

## Controversy and future science

The main unresolved issue is **which variant causes the effect**. The lead SNP is intergenic and likely tags a causal variant; the *RALY* exon-6 repeat is a strong candidate but could not be separated from the GWAS signal because of high linkage disequilibrium. [page:0]

Future work needs:

- Larger, multi-breed cohorts.
- Objective spectrophotometric or color-calibrated phenotyping.
- Long-read or haplotype-resolved assembly of the difficult ECA22 region.
- Functional testing of *RALY* and *ASIP* regulatory variants.
- Tests for pleiotropic effects on metabolism, behavior, and health. [page:0]

EARLIER NOTE (2026-10-12, before round four)
--------------------------------------------

What it is modelling
--------------------

A genome-wide association study of 126 genetically confirmed bay horses found a ~0.5 Mb region on equine chromosome 22, immediately upstream of ASIP and taking in the neighbouring RALY gene, very strongly associated with the continuum from light bay (black confined to mane, tail, lower legs and ear margins) through to near-black bay (black over most of the body with only small red areas left). It is by a wide margin the strongest known signal for bay shade - much stronger than the ordinary A/a test.

The causal mutation has not been identified. The lead marker sits in a high-linkage block and nobody has yet disentangled whether the functional variant is in ASIP, in RALY, or in a regulatory element controlling one or both. The leading biological guess is a regulatory variant that alters when, where or how strongly ASIP is expressed: broader ASIP activity inhibits MC1R over more of the horse and biases it redder; narrower activity lets eumelanin cover more of it.

So there is no allele to name, and this gene does not pretend there is. What it models is the thing the association actually describes: a haplotype dosage, as three alleles worth -1.5, 0 and +1.5, summed.

Why there is no At
------------------

The model you will see everywhere else
Most horse-colour material uses an agouti allelic series: A+ wild bay, A bay, At seal brown, a black. It is tidy, it is easy to teach, and it is not a validated equine explanation.

A tan-point at allele is genuinely mapped in several other mammals, and the horse version was borrowed from them by analogy. But the commercial equine agouti test distinguishes A from the recessive 11 bp deletion a and nothing else, and seal-brown-looking horses test as ordinary A/A or A/a - genetically bay at that locus. The chromosome-22 region above is a far stronger modern lead.

So this data does not ship At as a real allele. There were three honest options - give each shade a made-up allele, ship At and label it a simplification, or model a shade locus plus the dosage terms. This is the third.
