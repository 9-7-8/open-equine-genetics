PLOD1 in Horses: Complete Locus Overview
PLOD1 (procollagen-lysine, 2-oxoglutarate 5-dioxygenase 1) is a connective-tissue gene, not a coat-color locus. In horses, its best-established disease allele causes Fragile Foal Syndrome type 1 (FFS)—formerly called Warmblood Fragile Foal Syndrome (WFFS)—a fatal autosomal-recessive collagen defect.[[ceh.vetmed.ucdavis](https://ceh.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/health-topics/warmblood-fragile-foal-syndrome-type-1-wffs)][[vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/research/wffs-breed-distribution)]

What PLOD1 does
---------------

PLOD1 encodes lysyl hydroxylase 1, an enzyme that hydroxylates lysine residues in collagen. Those hydroxylysine sites are needed for stable collagen cross-links, which give skin, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels, and other connective tissues their strength and elasticity. [18][2]

In horses, the clinically important variant is:

Allele | Molecular change | Effect
N / normal | Wild-type PLOD1 | Normal collagen cross-linking
FFS / WFFS | c.2032G>A, p.Gly678Arg | Impaired enzyme function; two copies cause Fragile Foal Syndrome type 1

The variant is inherited autosomal recessively: one copy generally produces no known clinical signs, while two copies produce a severe, untreatable neonatal connective-tissue disorder. [16][18]

Effect on red and black pigment
-------------------------------

PLOD1 has no established effect on red or black pigment. It does not determine whether a horse makes eumelanin (black/brown pigment) or pheomelanin (red/yellow pigment), nor does it dilute, restrict, or pattern either pigment type. [16][12]

Base coat color is governed primarily by:

- MC1R / Extension: E permits black pigment; e results in chestnut/red base color. [12]
- ASIP / Agouti: A restricts black pigment to the points, producing bay on an E-based horse; a allows uniform black expression. [12]

A PLOD1 carrier or affected foal can therefore occur on any genetically possible base color—black, bay, chestnut, or any color produced by dilution or white-pattern genes. Coat color and PLOD1 status should be treated as independent traits in a breeding simulator.

Known alleles and speculation
-----------------------------

Well established
................

- N (normal): The ordinary functional allele.
- FFS (c.2032G>A): A pathogenic missense variant associated with FFS/WFFS. UC Davis offers a direct DNA test for it. [16][18]

What is not established
.......................

There is no validated menu of multiple PLOD1 “coat” or “conformation” alleles in horses analogous to, for example, cream, dun, or agouti alleles. The equine PLOD1 story is dominated by one pathogenic variant rather than a polymorphic visual trait system. [16][18]

Speculative or unresolved possibilities include:

- Early embryonic loss in homozygotes: UC Davis notes that homozygosity may contribute to early pregnancy loss, but says more research is needed. [16]
- Subclinical effects in carriers: Current UC Davis guidance states that carriers have an excellent prognosis and no known health problems associated with carrier status. [16]
- Other PLOD1 variants: Other variants may exist, but they are not established equine coat-color, performance, or conformation alleles. Any claim that PLOD1 produces a visible “type” should be treated as unsupported.

Effect on body structure and performance
----------------------------------------

Because PLOD1 affects collagen cross-linking, its major proven effect is on connective-tissue integrity, especially in homozygous affected foals. [16][18]

Trait | Established PLOD1 effect? | Notes
Skin | Yes, in FFS/FFS foals | Thin, hyperextensible, fragile skin and mucous membranes; open lesions. [16]
Joints / ligaments | Yes, in FFS foals | Hyperextensible limb joints. [16]
Ears | Yes, in FFS foals | Floppy ears have been described. [16]
Fetal/neonatal development | Yes, in FFS foals | Hydrops, hematomas, seromas, subcutaneous emphysema, premature birth, and nonviability can occur. [16]
Height | No established effect | No evidence that PLOD1 genotype determines equine height.
Speed | No established effect | A Thoroughbred study found the FFS allele was not associated with catastrophic breakdown; it does not establish a speed effect either. [14]
Muscle mass or function | No established direct effect | PLOD1 acts through collagen, not muscle pigment or muscle-fiber type.
Bone structure | No established equine phenotype | Collagen is important to bone, but the equine FFS variant is characterized as a connective-tissue/dermal and joint defect, not a validated bone-shape locus. [16][18]
Hoof shape or quality | No established effect | Not a recognized cause of hoof conformation traits.
Feathering | No effect | Feathering is unrelated to PLOD1.
Mane or tail color | No effect | Mane and tail pigment follow the same pigment-genetic systems as body coat, not PLOD1.
White markings or patterns | No established effect | PLOD1 is not a white-pattern or spotting locus.

Clinical signs of two copies
----------------------------

Foals with FFS/FFS are abnormal from birth. UC Davis describes:

- Abnormally thin, fragile, hyperextensible skin and mucous membranes.
- Skin lesions, often developing from friction or pressure, sometimes in utero.
- Hyperextensible limb joints.
- Floppy ears.
- Hematomas, seromas, hydrops, and subcutaneous emphysema.
- Premature birth; affected foals may be stillborn, nonviable, or euthanized shortly after birth. [16]

There is no treatment or cure; the prognosis for affected foals is grave. [16]

Inheritance and breeding
------------------------

Mating | Expected foal outcomes
N/N × N/N | 100% N/N
N/N × N/FFS | 50% N/N, 50% N/FFS
N/FFS × N/FFS | 25% N/N, 50% N/FFS, 25% FFS/FFS
N/FFS × FFS/FFS | 50% N/FFS, 50% FFS/FFS
FFS/FFS × FFS/FFS | 100% FFS/FFS

Two carrier parents have a 25% chance of producing an affected foal per pregnancy. Breeding a carrier to a normal horse will not produce an affected foal, but can produce carriers 50% of the time. [16]

Breeds where it is found
------------------------

FFS was first recognized in Warmbloods, and the allele is most common in Warmblood/sport-horse populations. UC Davis reports an average carrier frequency of about 11% across Warmbloods tested, with estimates of 9–11% in Warmblood horses more broadly. [16][18]

Breed group | Finding
Warmbloods | Primary affected population; allele detected in 17 of 19 tested Warmblood breeds. [18]
Hanoverian | Among the highest reported carrier frequencies, about 17.3%. [18]
Danish Warmblood | Also about 17.3% carrier frequency. [18]
Thoroughbred | Low frequency: 17 of 716 horses were carriers, or 2.4%; allele frequency about 1.2%. [18][14]
Knabstrupper | Allele detected: 3 of 46 tested. [18]
Haflinger | Allele detected: 2 of 48 tested. [18]
American Sport Pony | Allele detected: 1 of 12 tested. [18]
Arabian | Not detected in 302 tested Arabians; evidence does not support an Arabian origin. [18]

UC Davis recommends testing all Warmblood/sport-horse populations and non-Warmblood breeds in which the allele has been detected. [16]

Controversy and future science
------------------------------

The main historical controversy involved whether the PLOD1 FFS allele contributed to catastrophic breakdown injuries in Thoroughbreds. A study of 716 Thoroughbreds found the allele at low frequency and concluded it was not a genetic risk factor for catastrophic breakdown; only one breakdown case carried the allele. [14]

Other open questions include:

- Whether homozygous FFS pregnancies sometimes end in early embryonic loss rather than a recognizable affected foal. [16]
- The exact historical origin and founder spread of c.2032G>A; UC Davis reports that its origin remains unknown and that an Arabian origin is unlikely. [18]
- Whether other PLOD1 or collagen-pathway variants contribute to subtle differences in skin, tendon, or joint quality. This remains a research question, not an established breeding trait.

Simulator design notes
----------------------

For a horse-breeding simulation, model PLOD1 as a health/structural trait, not a color modifier:

- Use two alleles: N and FFS.
- N/FFS: visually normal carrier; no coat, height, speed, hoof, feathering, mane, or tail-color effect.
- FFS/FFS: lethal or nonviable neonatal phenotype, with fragile skin, joint laxity, and possible fetal loss.
- Keep PLOD1 independent of MC1R, ASIP, dilution loci, white-pattern loci, and quantitative conformation traits unless you deliberately add a house-rule mechanic.
- If modeling real-world prevalence, make the allele uncommon outside Warmblood-derived populations and rare in Thoroughbreds. [16][18]

At a glance
-----------

Gene | PLOD1 - procollagen-lysine, 2-oxoglutarate 5-dioxygenase 1
Protein | Lysyl hydroxylase 1 (LH1), needed for normal collagen maturation and cross-linking
Chromosome | Equine chromosome 3 (ECA3); EquCab3.0 NC_009145.3:g.39927817C>T
Variant | PLOD1:c.2032G>A, p.Gly678Arg (p.G678R) - missense
Disorder | Warmblood fragile foal syndrome type 1 (WFFS / FFS); an Ehlers-Danlos-like syndrome
Inheritance | Autosomal recessive
Homozygote | Severe congenital connective-tissue failure; non-viable or fatally compromised
Heterozygote | Clinically normal carrier on present evidence
Coat | No known effect

The genomic notation reads C>T while the transcript reads G>A. That is not an inconsistency: PLOD1 is transcribed from the opposite DNA strand, so the same physical event is written differently depending on which reference you are counting from. OMIA 001982 (https://omia.org/OMIA001982/9796/)

What breaks
-----------

PLOD1 encodes lysyl hydroxylase 1, active during collagen production. Collagen is the tensile-strength protein of skin, tendon, ligament, fascia, blood-vessel wall and joint capsule. Before collagen becomes a working fibre, selected lysine residues in the collagen chains have to be hydroxylated; those modified residues then provide the sites for glycosylation and, above all, for the orderly intermolecular cross-links that hold the fibril together.

Lose that step and the chain runs: poor lysine hydroxylation -> abnormal cross-linking -> mechanically weak connective tissue. It is the same biology as kyphoscoliotic Ehlers-Danlos syndrome from PLOD1 deficiency in people - faulty collagen maturation, and tissue that cannot take ordinary mechanical stress. PMC8524838 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8524838/)

The substitution is glycine to arginine at residue 678. Glycine is very small and flexible; arginine is large and positively charged. In an enzyme whose three-dimensional shape is what lets it recognise a collagen substrate, that is a substitution with an obvious way to do damage.

The case for c.2032G>A being causal: affected foals tested so far have been homozygous, parents fit the carrier pattern, the clinical signs match loss of a collagen-processing enzyme, the phenotype parallels the human PLOD1 disease, and heterozygotes in reported studies were clinically normal. One nuance worth carrying: the variant was first described in a patent application rather than a peer-reviewed molecular-discovery paper, with later clinical, population and genetic work reinforcing the association. Residual enzyme activity in affected equine tissue has never been comprehensively quantified the way it has for some human PLOD1 disorders.

What an affected foal looks like
--------------------------------

The hallmark is profound skin fragility - skin so thin and hyperextensible that it tears under ordinary handling, ordinary movement, or the mechanical forces of birth itself. Reported findings include large open lesions on the limbs, neck, abdomen, perineum or tail region; loose, easily torn skin; areas of missing skin or skin separation; large wounds from minimal trauma; and exposed underlying tissue in severe cases.

That is not a dermatological defect. Skin is simply the most visible surface of a body-wide connective-tissue disorder. Affected foals may also have lax and hyperextensible joints, limb deformity or instability, flexural deformity, and in some cases abnormal spinal-canal or vertebral development. Severe cases reach the internal tissues: abdominal-wall defects, small-intestinal eventration, aortic perforating lesions.

Because the foals are generally non-viable, there is no evidence about adult conformation, soundness or athletic development in a ffs/ffs horse. The genotype's phenotype is neonatal failure, not a recognisable adult "fragile build".

Where the mod and the pathology agree, and where they don't
The mod makes this LETHAL_AT_BIRTH, which is the documented presentation: a non-viable term foal is the best-attested outcome. Earlier fetal loss is biologically plausible and has been proposed, but has not been established as the principal outcome, so the conception path is left to MET. What the mod cannot show is that not every affected foal has dramatic skin lesions - some are subtle, which is why testing is appropriate in an unexplained stillbirth even without the classic tearing.

The carrier question, which is where the misinformation lives
-------------------------------------------------------------

What is supported. Heterozygotes are generally described as clinically unaffected. No published study has confirmed a carrier syndrome of fragile skin, joint laxity, tendon weakness, poor growth, reduced soundness or impaired performance.

The most useful evidence on athletic risk is a 716-horse Thoroughbred case-control study. It found 17 carriers - 2.4% - and no homozygotes, and carrier and allele frequencies did not differ significantly between horses with catastrophic musculoskeletal breakdown and multiple control groups, including older racehorses and racehorses from the same track and season. The conclusion was that this variant is not a genetic risk factor for catastrophic breakdown in Thoroughbreds. PMC7062577 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7062577/)

That is not a proof that no carrier ever tears a tendon - those are common multifactorial problems. It is that the published evidence does not support treating carrier status as a demonstrated cause of unsoundness.

What is possible but unproven. Breeders have wondered whether one copy subtly changes collagen quality, either downward (injury susceptibility) or upward (more elasticity, better movement, a particular sport-horse type). Both are speculative. The Irish population study notes that such theories exist and that no study has confirmed a collagen-defective condition in heterozygotes. It also notes reported statistical correlations between the variant and estimated breeding values for gaits and rideability in one Warmblood analysis - which is not proof of anything, since correlations like that arise readily from breed-family structure, influential sires, selection history, linked genes, management and uneven sampling.

The one-sentence version
A WFFS carrier is not currently known to be more athletic, less athletic, more fragile or less sound than a non-carrier. Its established significance is reproductive risk when bred to another carrier - which is exactly the weight the mod gives it: the carrier row costs nothing at all, and the whole cost of the locus sits behind a pairing.

Inheritance
-----------

mating | per conception | consequence
N/N x N/N | 100% clear | no carriers, no affected foals
N/N x N/ffs | 50% clear, 50% carrier | no affected foals
N/ffs x N/ffs | 25% / 50% / 25% | one in four affected, per conception
N/ffs x ffs/ffs | 50% carrier, 50% affected | theoretical - affected foals are non-viable
N/N x ffs/ffs | 100% carrier | theoretical

Not sex-linked; colts and fillies carry and are affected equally. The 25% is per pregnancy, not a promise about four of them. The preventive step is one line long: do not mate two carriers. A carrier bred to a tested-clear mate cannot produce an affected foal and keeps whatever else it was worth keeping for.

The mod's rate, and why this gene has the highest one
WILD_CARRIER_PERCENT is 2.6 - the highest of the mod's four foal lethals. That ordering is the right shape for what is on this page: the real allele is concentrated in the sport-horse populations, hiding inside a large number of otherwise excellent animals rather than in a small isolated pocket. The absolute number is world-wide-founder scale, not Warmblood scale.

Where the allele came from - unsolved
-------------------------------------

Several proposed founders have been tested and did not hold up. The influential English Thoroughbred stallion Dark Ronald (1905-1928) was suggested from pedigree patterns, but testing of preserved skin exhibits found him homozygous normal at the variant site. The Arabian stallion Bairactar Or. Ar. (1813-1838) was also proposed, and historical DNA testing did not support him either. Pedigree analysis of known carriers identified a Hanoverian stallion born in 1861 as the most recent common ancestor of one carrier group - a point of convergence in those pedigrees, not necessarily where the mutation arose.

The current reading is that WFFS was enriched by historical Warmblood breeding and a few influential lines, possibly with older Thoroughbred contribution, and that the horse in which c.2032G>A first appeared remains unknown.

What is settled, and what is not
--------------------------------

Well established. PLOD1 encodes an enzyme essential for collagen cross-linking; c.2032G>A p.Gly678Arg causes WFFS; the locus is on ECA3; inheritance is autosomal recessive; homozygotes have a severe congenital systemic connective-tissue disorder with thin fragile hyperextensible skin, wounds and skin loss, joint and limb abnormalities and sometimes abdominal, intestinal, vascular or spinal involvement; affected foals are usually stillborn, non-viable or euthanised; carriers are clinically normal in the literature; the allele is concentrated in Warmbloods and present at low frequency in Thoroughbreds.

Plausible but unsettled. Whether some affected pregnancies are lost earlier in gestation; the full biochemical signature of the collagen defect in affected horses; whether all affected foals show obvious skin lesions at birth (they do not); the founder and date of origin; and the true carrier frequency inside every national population, family and registry.

Not supported. That carrier status causes catastrophic breakdown in Thoroughbreds; that carriers have a predictable weak-skin, weak-tendon, loose-joint or fragile-bone syndrome; that carriers are inherently faster, better jumpers, more elastic movers or more rideable; that the variant predicts coat colour, white markings or eye colour; or that carriers should automatically be removed from breeding merely for carrying it.
