# Flaxen (light mane and tail on chestnut)

## Bottom line

**No gene, locus, causal variant, or validated DNA test for the “flaxen” phenotype has been published.** Flaxen therefore remains a phenotypic descriptor—not an established Mendelian locus—despite its long-standing use as a hypothetical locus in horse-color models. UC Davis’s Veterinary Genetics Laboratory does not list a flaxen test among its coat-color tests, while it does list tests for look-alike traits such as silver, champagne, and mushroom. [web:22][web:19][web:20][web:18]

## What “flaxen” means

Flaxen describes a chestnut horse (\(e/e\) at *MC1R*/Extension) whose mane and tail are conspicuously lighter than the red body coat—commonly pale yellow, cream, near-white, or sometimes mixed light and dark hairs. It is a **mane-and-tail modification of red pigment**, not a separate base color. [web:5][web:7]

It must be distinguished from several genetically identified traits that can also produce a light mane and tail:

| Trait | Gene / variant | Effect relevant to flaxen look-alikes | Inheritance | UC Davis source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silver | *PMEL17*, exon 11 missense variant | Lightens black/brown eumelanin; produces flaxen or silver-gray mane/tail on black or bay bases, but has no visible coat effect on chestnuts | Autosomal dominant | [web:19] |
| Champagne | *SLC36A1* c.188C>G | On chestnut, produces gold body and often a flaxen mane/tail | Autosomal dominant | [web:20] |
| Mushroom | *MFSD12* frameshift variant | Sepia body dilution in chestnut Shetland-type ponies, often with flaxen mane/tail | Autosomal recessive | [web:18] |
| Cream | *MATP*/*SLC45A2* | One copy on chestnut gives palomino: gold body with pale mane/tail | Incomplete dominant | [web:5] |

Thus, a “flaxen” phenotype should be assigned only after excluding known dilutions—especially palomino, champagne, silver on a non-chestnut parent, and mushroom where relevant.

## Established facts

- Flaxen is observed only in expression on a **chestnut/red base**; it is not a visible phenotype on black or bay horses because those bases have black rather than red mane and tail hair. [web:7][web:32]
- Chestnut itself is well established as \(e/e\) at *MC1R*; *MC1R* controls whether black pigment can be produced, and UC Davis recognizes \(E\), \(e\), and \(e^a\) molecular alleles. [web:5]
- The expression is **variable**: some chestnuts are only mildly flaxen, while others have very pale manes and tails; this variability is inconsistent with a simple, fully penetrant one-locus trait. [web:7]
- Breeders’ observations, particularly in Morgan horses, support a **heritable** component: flaxen chestnuts bred to flaxen chestnuts have often produced flaxen chestnuts, and the trait can appear to “hide” in non-chestnut relatives and reappear in chestnut descendants. [web:7][web:31][web:32]
- No commercial or peer-reviewed molecular test identifies a flaxen genotype; phenotype is assessed visually after excluding known dilution loci. [web:22][web:31][web:32]

## What is not established

- **No causal mutation has been published.** I found no primary paper identifying a flaxen gene, SNP, indel, structural variant, or diagnostic haplotype.
- **No official locus symbol or allele symbol is established.** Older hobby and breed sources use labels such as \(F/f\) or \(F^f\), but these are informal model conventions, not validated molecular nomenclature. [web:7][web:9]
- **No robust heritability estimate** with sample size, population, and publication year appears to have been published. Claims that flaxen is recessive rest chiefly on pedigree and breeding observations, especially in Morgans—not on a segregation analysis with a published statistical model. [web:7][web:31][web:32]
- **No verified candidate region.** Some non-peer-reviewed sources mention a candidate region on horse chromosome 14, but I could not verify this in a primary, peer-reviewed flaxen study; it should not be treated as established. [web:13]
- Because no locus has been mapped, **no genomic coordinate on EquCab2 or EquCab3 can be given** for flaxen.

## Inheritance: best current interpretation

The safest scientific position is:

- **Heritable:** yes, supported by family and breed observations.
- **Mode of inheritance:** unresolved.
- **Most useful working hypothesis:** recessive or quasi-recessive major-effect factor, modified by additional genetic and possibly developmental/environmental variation.
- **Alternative hypothesis:** polygenic/oligogenic control of red-hair pigment distribution in mane and tail.

A simple recessive model explains why two visibly flaxen chestnuts tend to produce flaxen offspring, but it does not by itself explain the wide range of intensity or why mildly flaxen chestnuts can produce strongly flaxen offspring. [web:7]

## Simulator implementation

For a hyperrealistic simulation, keep flaxen as a **separate hidden locus**, but do not present it to players as scientifically proven.

Recommended model:

```text
FLX locus
FLX/FLX = strong flaxen expression on chestnut
FLX/flx  = carrier; no or minimal visible expression
flx/flx  = strong flaxen expression on chestnut
```

Then add an expression modifier:

```text
flaxen_intensity = base_FLX_effect
                  + polygenic_modifier_1
                  + polygenic_modifier_2
                  + stochastic_developmental_noise
```

Practical rules:

- Express flaxen only when the horse is \(e/e\) at Extension.
- Do not let flaxen change body color; alter only mane, tail, and optionally light feathering.
- Use a continuous 0–1 flaxen-intensity value rather than a binary on/off state.
- Let \(FLX/FLX\) and \(flx/flx\) produce high expression, while \(FLX/flx\) produces low, zero, or context-dependent expression; this preserves the breeder-observed recessive tendency without claiming certainty.
- Prevent flaxen from being visually assigned when cream, champagne, silver, or mushroom better explains the phenotype.
- In the UI, label it “flaxen phenotype (genetic basis unresolved)” rather than “the flaxen gene.”

This gives believable breeding outcomes while remaining honest about the missing molecular evidence.

## Breeds and prevalence

Flaxen is especially associated with **Morgan horses**, where it is relatively common and has been discussed in breed-color resources. [web:31][web:32] It is also seen in many other breeds with chestnut horses, but no reliable breed-level allele frequency, carrier frequency, sample size, or survey year can be cited because no validated genotype exists.

Do **not** confuse flaxen with silver: silver is common in Rocky Mountain Horses and related breeds, Shetland Ponies, Icelandic Horses, and Morgans, and it acts on black pigment rather than red. [web:19]

## Effects outside mane and tail

There is no evidence that a distinct flaxen locus affects height, speed, muscle, bone structure, hoof shape, or systemic traits. The established phenotype concerns lightening of red terminal hair—mane and tail, and sometimes feathering—but even feathering involvement is observational rather than genetically mapped. [web:7][web:10]

Mane and tail color is the relevant trait; flaxen does **not** create a black mane/tail and does not act on eumelanin. Silver, by contrast, acts on black/brown pigment and can lighten the mane and tail of bay or black horses. [web:19]

## Future science

A convincing flaxen study would need:

1. A large, phenotyped cohort of chestnuts, ideally Morgans plus another flaxen-rich breed.
2. Standardized scoring of mane/tail lightness, ideally blinded and repeated across age classes.
3. Whole-genome sequencing or dense SNP genotyping plus family-based linkage/GWAS.
4. Replication in an independent population.
5. Exclusion or stratification for cream, champagne, silver, mushroom, gray, and sooty/polygenic body-shade effects.

Until then, “flaxen” should remain a **phenotype-level, heritable but genetically unresolved trait**.

It is a phenotype, and this is a simulation of one
--------------------------------------------------

There is no identified flaxen mutation and no DNA test for it. That is the single most important fact about this gene and the reason it is shaped the way it is.

Breeders have modelled flaxen as a simple recessive for a century, written f/f or fl/fl, on pedigree evidence - flaxen bred to flaxen tends to give flaxen, and Morgan pedigrees in particular were studied for exactly this. That is a useful breeding observation and it is not proof of a locus. The veterinary-genetics resources list no flaxen variant and no commercial test, and they are explicit that a great deal of equine shade variation remains unexplained.

Model | Status
Dominant | No identified allele one copy of which reliably produces flaxen.
Recessive | Historical and pedigree support. Unconfirmed.
Codominant | No validated heterozygote distinct from either homozygote.
Incomplete dominant | No demonstrated one-copy-versus-two dose series, unlike cream.
Polygenic | The best fit for what is actually observed: the degree of lightening varies enormously, and mane and tail colour move with age, season, sun, nutrition and clipping.

So this locus is a simulated polygenic modifier, and its own description says so. It is the same shape the bay shade score takes, and for the same reason: a graded, heritable, additive quantity is what the evidence describes, and one locus with a dosage plus an expression roll says that in a genotype a reader can actually follow. Three to six separate polygenes would say it too, at the cost of six loci nobody could reason about.
