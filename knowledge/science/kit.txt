KIT (white spotting) - science notes
(Copied 2026-10-12 from the Science tab of the Procedural Horse Coats wiki page for this gene.
Written for that project: it may mention how a game models the gene. Not yet audited.)

Genetic identity
----------------

Gene | KIT - a receptor tyrosine kinase, a cell-surface signalling protein
Chromosome | Equine chromosome 3 (ECA3)
Ligand | KITL/SCF, stem-cell factor
Variants | Dozens, of every molecular class - missense, nonsense, splice, small deletion, gross deletion, frameshift and regulatory
Inheritance | Autosomal, dominant to incompletely dominant, with strongly variable expressivity

This locus does not choose red versus black. It determines where pigment-producing cells successfully reach and persist, so it makes areas unpigmented - white hair over pink skin - while the base genotype still colours whatever is left. PMC10854722 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

The mechanism is a migration failure
------------------------------------

The cell that matters is the melanoblast: a neural-crest-derived precursor that migrates during embryonic development and matures into a melanocyte. KIT signalling, driven by its ligand, supports the survival, proliferation, migration and differentiation of that lineage, and interacts with MITF, a major pigment-cell transcription factor. Reduce or alter KIT activity and some regions are never colonised; those regions grow white hair over pink skin.

MC1R, ASIP and modifiers -> which pigment, and what shade
KIT and the other spotting genes -> where there is no pigment cell at all

So a KIT-patterned chestnut is red where it is coloured, a KIT-patterned black is black there, and a bay keeps its black points wherever melanocytes survived. An all-white KIT horse is not albino - true albinism is not an established viable equine category. It retains ordinary pigment genetics in whatever regions still have melanocytes, and its eyes are generally normally pigmented. That is also why base colour becomes impossible to read visually on a maximal-white horse: the programme is there with almost no hair left to display it.

What is at this locus, and what only looks like it is
-----------------------------------------------------

This is the distinction the whole restructure turns on. Only alleles at exactly the same locus share a gene. SB1 and every numbered W are defined KIT variants, so they compete for the same two chromosome slots. Two patterns that sit right beside them do not:

Pattern | What it actually is | Where it lives here
Sabino 1, the numbered W series | KIT variants | this gene
Tobiano | a large inversion near / downstream of KIT, thought to disrupt its regulation rather than its coding sequence | its own gene
Roan | maps to the region; causal change unresolved, breed-dependent | its own gene
Splash white | MITF and PAX3 - different chromosomes entirely | two genes
Frame overo | EDNRB | its own gene

So a tobiano can also carry W20, and a dominant white can be a roan underneath - both of which real horses are, and neither of which the old single "white" gene could say.

The W series is not one mutation with strength levels
-----------------------------------------------------

Each numbered W is a distinct DNA change, usually founded in one family or breed, and the numbers reflect discovery order rather than severity. They fall into rough phenotype groups:

Group | Typical result
High-impact, all-white tendency | One copy often produces extremely extensive depigmentation, or an all-white pink-skinned horse
Sabino-like to very extensive | Variable leg, face and belly white with jagged edges; some heterozygotes are near-white
Mild "booster" variants - W20, W32, W34, W35 | Little or no visible spotting alone; reliably increase white beside another variant

W20 is the most misunderstood variant in horse colour genetics, pulled between two wrong poles. It is not meaningless - the association with increased spotting is real - and it is not a guaranteed white booster either. The accurate description is a probabilistic white-patterning enhancer: a W20 horse can be solid-looking, ordinary-marked, or sabino-like, and a flashy carrier may owe part of that to other loci entirely. W35 makes the same point from the molecular side: it is a 5′UTR regulatory variant, so KIT expression can be altered without touching the protein sequence.

SB1 is a well-characterised incompletely dominant allele - an intron-16 splice-site variant causing partial exon 17 skipping. One copy gives uneven stockings, face white, belly spotting and roaned or speckled jagged borders; two are viable and usually nearly or completely white. Note that "sabino" the look is far broader than SB1 the allele: many visually sabino horses are SB1-negative, which disproves the label and not the phenotype.

Why two horses with one genotype look different
-----------------------------------------------

- Stochastic embryonic migration. If reduced signalling leaves development near a threshold, chance differences in how many melanoblasts reach each region produce markedly different horses from the same genotype. This is the big one.
- Allele severity. A nonsense, frameshift, large deletion or critical splice disruption usually outweighs a subtle missense or regulatory change.
- Dosage, notably for SB1 and the viable mild variants.
- Allelic combinations, including more than two named variants - see below.
- Other spotting genes, and background genetics, breed selection and photograph-based phenotype scoring.

The recommended implementation is the one this gene uses
The review's advice for a breeding simulator is to treat white extent as a probability distribution conditional on genotype rather than a fixed pattern sprite - a major allele with a high probability of near-total white, a booster homozygote with a broad distribution centred much lower, and combinations shifting the distribution upward without guaranteeing a geometry. That is what the combination table plus a per-horse strength roll does here, and why every outcome is the same sabino shape at a different strength rather than a set of drawings.

Multi-variant haplotypes, and the two-slot problem
--------------------------------------------------

A common belief is that a horse cannot carry more than two KIT white variants because it has two copies of chromosome 3. That is wrong. One chromosome can carry a haplotype containing several named variants. W22 is consistently reported linked with W20; viable multi-variant haplotypes such as W32W35, W34W35, W20W32, W32SB1 and W19W34W35 are documented; and in a dataset of 8,334 horses, 434 carried three or more KIT variants, with some carrying six. PMC10854588 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854588/)

The consequences are real for breeders: a report reading "W20/W22" hides phase - whether the two sit on one chromosome or opposite ones - and phase decides what the horse transmits. Good reporting writes W20W22/n when it knows. It also means a rare strong allele beside mild ones is not automatically lethal: viability depends on the functional total of the two chromosome copies, not on counting labels.

Health
------

KIT is not pigment-only biology. It has developmental roles in melanoblasts, blood-cell development, mast cells, germ-cell biology and the interstitial cells of Cajal in the gut. At the extreme, near-complete loss of KIT function is expected to be developmentally catastrophic, with prenatal or perinatal lethality and anemia in comparative systems - which is the biological reason many high-impact equine variants are suspected nonviable in a damaging two-copy combination. PMC2065884 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2065884/)

What is documented in horses is narrower and more reassuring: SB1/SB1 is viable and healthy; tobiano homozygotes are viable and fertile; W20, W32, W34, W35, W19 and W15 have all been seen homozygous. No KIT-W syndrome comparable to lethal white foal syndrome has been established for heterozygous white-patterned horses.

The one genuine everyday consequence is pink skin under white, which raises sun sensitivity - a predictable result of missing melanin rather than a KIT disease, but a real management burden. And KIT is emphatically not responsible for the health risks attached to other white patterns: lethal white is EDNRB, deafness risk belongs to the splash alleles, night blindness to leopard complex, and melanoma to grey.

"Homozygous lethal" needs reading carefully
-------------------------------------------

The review lists a long set of W alleles as not observed homozygously and predicted homozygous lethal. The supporting arguments are good ones: no living homozygotes, disrupted protein domains, historical white x white matings yielding roughly 2:1 rather than 3:1 live-born ratios, and the comparative lethality of complete KIT loss.

But "not observed" is not "proven lethal", and the caveats matter. Many W alleles are rare and founder-restricted, so there may simply never have been enough carrier-to-carrier matings. The W label groups variants of radically different molecular impact. And some alleles once presumed lethal turned up in viable homozygotes - W15 is the standing example. Published datasets rarely carry systematic fertility, pregnancy-loss or hematologic follow-up.

Breeds and frequency
--------------------

Breed distribution is the wrong frame for this locus. Most numbered variants are founder-line events, often traced to a single de-novo animal and confined to its descendants, so a variant is where its founder's line went rather than where a breed is. The boosters are the exception and are widespread. A negative panel therefore means negative for the alleles on that panel, not negative for KIT - commercial testing lags discovery, and an unexplained white horse may carry an untested KIT change, a structural variant the assay cannot see, a non-KIT white gene, or several modifiers at once.

What is settled, and what is not
--------------------------------

Claim | Status
KIT controls melanocyte distribution and survival, not pigment type | Established
Tobiano's inversion, SB1's splice variant, and many individual W mutations | Established
Several named variants can sit on one chromosome | Established, and routinely mis-reported
"Dominant white" describes the series well | Poor term. Some variants are subtle, incompletely dominant, or viable homozygous. KIT white spotting is preferred
Each high-impact W is lethal when homozygous | Suspected, allele by allele, not proven
Roan is a KIT allele | Too confident. It maps near the region in several populations; the causal change is unsettled and may be heterogeneous
A visually sabino horse carries SB1 | False. Sabino-like is a look, SB1 is a variant

Where the mod departs
---------------------

- Haplotype phase is not modelled, and cannot be. A horse here has two alleles at this locus and no more, because an allele pair is the model - so W20W22/n is inexpressible, and the linked pairs the literature documents become ordinary compound heterozygotes. This is the largest single departure on the page. Linkage is a system rather than a number, and the mod settled against it deliberately.
- The allele list runs past this source. The review tabulates the series to W35; this gene ships every published variant beyond it as well, from later reports, with the two numbers that were retracted left out. A reader comparing the two should not "correct" the gene down to the review - the review is one snapshot of a series that is still growing.
- W34's reported interaction with MC1R - possibly stronger on a chestnut background - is not modelled. "Possibly" is doing too much work in that sentence to buy an outcome with.
- Pigment lost with age is not modelled, on this locus or anywhere: aging is out of scope, so the variants that depigment progressively render as their adult state.
