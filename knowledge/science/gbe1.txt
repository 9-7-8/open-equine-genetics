- GBE1 is on chromosome 26 (26q12-q13). The variant is c.102C>A in exon 1.
- UC Davis estimates 8.3% carriers in Quarter Horses and 7.1% in Paint Horses; older broad
  estimates of roughly 10% in Quarter Horse, Paint and related bloodlines also appear.
- Asked which 2006 study gave allele frequencies of 4.1% and 3.6%, the answer names "the
  2006 Ward et al. study" as the report of the mutation and gives no carrier percentages
  or address for it. The question is still open.
- Affected foals may be aborted, stillborn, weak at birth, or die within months.

GBE1 is not a coat-color locus. In horses, it is best known for glycogen branching enzyme deficiency (GBED), a fatal autosomal-recessive metabolic disease concentrated in Quarter Horse–related breeds. UC Davis lists the normal allele as N and the disease allele as G; a foal needs two copies of G to be affected. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

Locus and function
------------------

GBE1 encodes glycogen branching enzyme, which creates branch points in glycogen—the body’s compact, rapidly mobilizable glucose-storage molecule. Without functional GBE1, tissues cannot form normally branched glycogen, so energy storage and release are severely impaired. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

The equine disease allele is a single-base substitution, c.102C>A, in exon 1. It creates a premature stop codon, p.Tyr34Ter (Y34*), so no functional glycogen-branching enzyme is produced. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

Alleles and inheritance
-----------------------

UC Davis genotype | Meaning | Breeding outcome
N/N | Normal; not a carrier | Cannot pass GBED to offspring.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)
N/G | Carrier; clinically unaffected | Passes G to about 50% of foals.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)
G/G | Affected | GBED is fatal; no viable adult affected horses.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

Two carriers produce, on average, 25% affected, 50% carriers, and 25% N/N foals. Because affected foals die before breeding, the disease allele is maintained only through clinically normal carriers. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

Clinical effects
----------------

GBED affects the fetus or newborn foal rather than producing a survivable adult phenotype. Reported outcomes include late-term abortion, stillbirth, weakness, seizures, muscle weakness, and early neonatal death; the abnormal glycogen storage especially compromises skeletal muscle, heart muscle, liver, and brain. [ceh.vetmed.ucdavis](https://ceh.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/health-topics/glycogen-branching-enzyme-deficiency-gbed)

Carrier horses (N/G) have roughly half normal GBE activity but are otherwise asymptomatic; there is no evidence that carrying one copy causes a visible coat, conformational, or performance phenotype. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198616/)

What it does not affect
-----------------------

GBE1 is a metabolic enzyme gene, not a pigment, structural, or performance-selection locus. There are no well-established equine GBE1 effects on:

- Red versus black pigment, bay/black/chestnut expression, mane or tail color, or dilution/pattern phenotypes.
- Height, speed, muscle mass, bone structure, hoof shape, or feathering.
- Any adult-onset visible trait; homozygous G/G foals do not survive to display adult traits. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

For a simulator, model GBE1 as a hidden health/lethality allele, not a visible trait: N/G is invisible, while G/G should result in abortion, stillbirth, or neonatal death rather than an adult horse.

Breeds and frequency
--------------------

GBED is found primarily in American Quarter Horses, American Paint Horses, and related Quarter Horse-lineage breeds; mixed-breed horses with Quarter Horse ancestry can also carry it. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

A major 2006 frequency study found mutant-allele frequencies of about 4.1% in registered Quarter Horses, 3.6% in Paint Horses, and 0% in Thoroughbreds. Carrier percentages are therefore much higher than allele frequencies—roughly 8–9% in Quarter Horse and Paint populations in later summaries. [merckvetmanual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/musculoskeletal-system/myopathies-in-horses/hereditary-and-congenital-myopathies-in-horses)

Established vs. speculative
---------------------------

Well established:

- Equine GBED is caused by GBE1 c.102C>A / p.Y34*. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)
- Inheritance is autosomal recessive. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)
- G/G is lethal in fetuses or neonates. [ceh.vetmed.ucdavis](https://ceh.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/health-topics/glycogen-branching-enzyme-deficiency-gbed)
- The mutation traces to a single founder and is especially relevant to Quarter Horse-related breeding programs. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17063718/)

Not established / should not be assumed:

- Additional pathogenic equine GBE1 alleles analogous to the many human GSD IV variants have not been established as routine equine disease alleles; the c.102C>A variant is the recognized equine test target. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)
- Claims that GBE1 carrier status changes athletic ability, muscle type, temperament, coat color, or conformation are speculation rather than validated phenotype associations. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)
- Gene-editing research has demonstrated correction of the mutation in equine cells, but this is experimental science, not an established breeding or clinical treatment. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198616/)

Testing and breeding use
------------------------

UC Davis offers a direct DNA test reporting N/N, N/G, or G/G. The practical breeding rule is simple: avoid N/G × N/G matings, because each such cross has a 25% chance of an affected foal. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

For a genetics simulation, use:

- Alleles: N (functional), G (GBED).
- Zygosity: N/N normal; N/G unaffected carrier; G/G embryonic/fetal or neonatal lethal.
- Visibility: none for carriers; no coat-color interaction.
- Population structure: founder effect in Quarter Horse-derived lines, with reduced but nonzero carrier frequency in related breeds and mixes. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)


EARLIER NOTES, FROM THE MOD'S WIKI
==================================
(Copied 2026-10-12 from the Science tab of the Procedural Horse Coats wiki page for this gene.
Written for that project: they say how a game models the gene. Where they disagree with
the research notes above, the notes above stand.)

What the enzyme does
--------------------

GBE1 is glucan (1,4-alpha-), branching enzyme 1. It encodes glycogen branching enzyme, which puts the α-1,6 branch points into an otherwise α-1,4-linked glycogen chain. The branching is the whole point of glycogen: it makes the molecule compact and soluble, and it creates a great many free ends from which glucose can be released at once. That matters most in the tissues with the highest and most variable energy demand - skeletal muscle, heart, liver and brain.

With no functional enzyme, cells build a poorly branched, amylopectin-like polyglucosan instead. It is a bad energy reserve and it accumulates. Affected foals show PAS-positive globular or crystalline intracellular inclusions in muscle, heart and liver, with branching-enzyme activity essentially absent. OMIA 000420 (https://omia.org/OMIA000420/9796/)

The equine disorder goes by several names - GBED, equine glycogen storage disease type IV, amylopectinosis. It is broadly homologous to human GBE1-associated GSD IV, which does not license assuming the clinical course of a different variant in a different species is the same.

The variant
-----------

Gene | GBE1
Chromosome | Equine chromosome 26, cytogenetically ECA26q12-q13. OMIA places the gene interval at NC_091709.1:15,099,930-15,345,666
Variant | c.102C>A; in EquCab3.0 coordinates NC_009169.3:g.8667651C>A
Protein | p.(Tyr34Ter) - p.Y34* or Y34X
Type | Nonsense / stop-gain, in exon 1
Consequence | A premature stop codon at amino acid 34, giving a severely truncated, non-functional protein
Classification | Pathogenic; rs3437568674
Inheritance | Autosomal recessive

Stopping at residue 34 is not a subtle activity-altering change. It is expected to abolish the enzyme outright, which is exactly what the biochemistry in affected foals shows. UC Davis VGL (https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/gbed)

Inheritance in a real population
--------------------------------

mating | expected foals | affected risk
clear x clear | 100% clear | 0%
clear x carrier | 50% clear, 50% carrier | 0%
carrier x carrier | 25% clear, 50% carrier, 25% affected | 25%
carrier x affected | 50% carrier, 50% affected | 50%
affected x affected | 100% affected | 100%

The last two rows are hypothetical in the real world - no affected horse survives to breed - and they are impossible in the mod for the same reason. Not sex-linked: colts and fillies are affected at equal rates. In testing nomenclature N/N is clear, N/G carrier, G/G affected; a carrier passes the allele to about half its foals and is not itself a diagnosis of muscle disease.

What happens to an affected foal
--------------------------------

Reported outcomes span late-term abortion, stillbirth, and live birth followed by profound weakness and poor muscle tone - persistent recumbency, hypothermia, flexural limb deformities, seizures, respiratory failure, cardiac failure, and death or euthanasia in early life. Laboratory findings can include leukopenia, intermittent hypoglycaemia, and raised CK, AST and GGT. The early course can look like ordinary neonatal septicaemia or pneumonia, which is why the DNA test is what makes the diagnosis.

The prognosis is grave and there is no treatment that touches the enzymatic defect. UC Davis reports that every known affected case died or was euthanised by five months, and that foals surviving to term typically die or are euthanised by 18 weeks on weakness alone.

Why the mod kills it at birth rather than at conception
Real GBED does both - it aborts late-term foals and it kills live ones over weeks. Either would have been defensible. MET owns the conception path deliberately and alone, so that one gene means the pairing yields nothing and everything else is born and lost where a player can watch it happen. The eighteen weeks are the part the mod cannot render: it has no age model (gap 1), so the foal is born, named, recorded in the pedigree, and dies in seconds. What is preserved is the thing that matters for breeding - the pairing tells you what both parents were.

Carriers are clinically normal
------------------------------

The best-supported conclusion is that N/G horses are unaffected. Early family studies found branching-enzyme activity in blood cells of dams and relatives at roughly 50% of control values - biologically consistent with carriage, and not associated with any recognised clinical phenotype.

Three things follow, and the third is the one that gets forgotten: a carrier is not an affected horse; carrier status does not mean the horse has PSSM1, RER, HYPP or any other muscle disorder; and a symptomatic adult carrier should not have its signs pinned on GBED just because the test came back N/G. The mod agrees - the carrier row costs nothing at all.

Not a coat, build or performance gene
-------------------------------------

There is no credible evidence that GBE1 controls or modifies base colour, dilution, white patterning, roan, grey, appaloosa patterning, mane and tail character, or coat texture and shedding. It sits on chromosome 26, away from the familiar pigment loci; where GBED tracks with a colour or a line, that is population history.

Nor is there a validated association between one carrier allele and mature height, bone length, frame, muscling, body mass, conformation or growth rate. For a G/G foal, "build" is affected the way grave illness affects build - weakness, poor tone, transient flexural deformity - which is disease, not evidence that GBE1 is a conformation gene.

Why the myth sounds reasonable
Glycogen obviously matters to exercise, so it is tempting to ask whether a GBE1 allele changes performance. The step from "this pathway matters for exercise" to "this disease allele predicts performance" is not one the evidence supports. Athletic traits are built from glucose handling, muscle metabolism, cardiovascular function, coordination, biomechanics, conditioning, feeding and a very large number of variants. This one is a recessive loss-of-function lethal. Disabling an engine's fuel-storage system is not a speed modification just because fuel matters in racing.

Breeds and frequency
--------------------

Established primarily in American Quarter Horses, American Paint Horses, and horses with relevant Quarter Horse or Paint ancestry. It was first characterised in Quarter Horse families and the same variant was later documented in Paints.

UC Davis estimates carrier frequency around 8.3% in Quarter Horses and 7.1% in Paints, and gives roughly 8-11% for the US Quarter Horse population overall, higher again in Western Pleasure and Cutting subgroups. AQHA requires the test for breeding stallions and includes it in the five-panel framework. These are population estimates; an individual programme can sit well away from them.

The mod's WILD_CARRIER_PERCENT is 2.0 - lower, because it describes every founder horse in the world rather than one breed with a bottleneck. A per-breed carrier weighting is the thing that would let this page's 8% and the mod's 2% be the same number seen from two distances.

What is settled, and what is not
--------------------------------

claim | status
c.102C>A / p.Y34* in GBE1 causes GBED | strongly established
autosomal recessive | strongly established
G/G foals have severe, typically fatal fetal or neonatal disease | strongly established
GBE1 maps to ECA26q12-q13 | established
carriers have about half-normal enzyme activity in some cells | supported by early family studies
carriers have a clinically important adult muscle or performance syndrome | not established
the allele improves speed, jump, endurance or build | no evidence
the allele causes coat or white-pattern variation | no evidence
this variant explains all early foal weakness | incorrect - differentials still matter
other equine GBE1 variants cause further distinct phenotypes | not established at testing-grade confidence

The nuance worth keeping: "GBE1 is metabolically important" is true and "p.Y34* is a lethal recessive" is true, and neither of them implies that ordinary variation at GBE1 explains ordinary variation in adult body type or athletic ability. That third claim would need population-scale genotype-phenotype work, and it does not have it.
