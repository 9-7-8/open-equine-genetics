Dun is a dominant, ancestral coat-color dilution controlled by the TBX3 gene on horse chromosome 8. One copy of the dun allele (D) lightens both red and black body pigment while leaving the mane, tail, lower legs, and head relatively undiluted, and it produces darker “primitive markings.” [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Locus and alleles
-----------------

The locus is commonly called the D locus or Dun locus, but its underlying gene is TBX3 (T-box transcription factor 3), mapped to equine chromosome 8. Three well-established alleles explain the major dun/non-dun phenotypes. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Allele | Also written | Dilution? | Primitive markings? | Inheritance behavior
D | Dun | Yes | Yes, typically | Dominant over nd1 and nd2; one copy is enough for dun  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
nd1 | Non-dun 1 | No | Often yes, variable | Dominant over nd2; can produce a dorsal stripe, leg barring, etc. without body dilution  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
nd2 | Non-dun 2 | No | No | Recessive to D and nd1; a 1,617-bp regulatory deletion  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Genotypes and phenotypes
........................

Genotype | Visual result | Breeding note
D/D | Dun, with primitive markings | Always passes D; every foal receives one dun allele  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
D/nd1 | Dun, with primitive markings | 50% chance of passing D; 50% chance of passing nd1  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
D/nd2 | Dun, with primitive markings | 50% chance of passing D; 50% chance of passing nd2  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
nd1/nd1 | Non-dun, but may show primitive markings | Always passes nd1  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
nd1/nd2 | Non-dun, but may show primitive markings | 50% chance of passing nd1; 50% chance of passing nd2  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
nd2/nd2 | Non-dun, no primitive markings from this locus | Always passes nd2; cannot produce a dun foal unless the other parent contributes D  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

There is no established dosage effect: D/D and D/nd1 or D/nd2 horses are described as having the same dun phenotype, unlike dilution genes where two copies can produce a stronger effect. [gluck.mgcafe.uky](https://gluck.mgcafe.uky.edu/color-testing)

Effect on red and black pigment
-------------------------------

Dun acts on the distribution of pigment within individual hairs, not by converting one base color into another. In a dun hair, pigment is deposited asymmetrically, leaving a lighter central zone and darker edges; this creates the diluted body color and dark primitive markings. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4731265/)

Base color | Dun expression | Common name
Chestnut / red base (ee) | Red body diluted to a sandy or reddish tone; red mane and tail remain relatively saturated | Red dun
Bay base (E_ A_) | Red body diluted; black points, mane, and tail remain dark | Bay dun, classic dun, or zebra dun
Black base (E_ aa) | Black body diluted to slate, mouse-gray, or smoky blue-gray; black mane and tail remain dark | Grullo / grulla, blue dun, or mouse dun

UC Davis summarizes the effect as lightening both red and black pigment while sparing the head, lower legs, mane, and tail from full dilution.  Thus, dun does not create a new base color: it modifies chestnut, bay, or black after the MC1R and ASIP base-color system has determined them. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

Primitive markings
------------------

The classic dun phenotype includes darker markings against the diluted body:

- Dorsal stripe along the spine  
- Leg barring, often called “zebra stripes”  
- Shoulder stripes or transverse markings  
- Facial masking or darker facial areas  
- Cobwebbing on the forehead  
- Darker mane/tail guard hairs in some horses  

These markings are part of the D phenotype, but their intensity and completeness vary substantially between individuals. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Importantly, primitive markings alone do not prove dun. Horses with nd1 are not dun-diluted but may show a dorsal stripe, leg barring, and other primitive markings; conversely, nd2/nd2 horses generally lack them. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

How the mutation works
----------------------

TBX3 is a transcription factor expressed in developing hair follicles. In dun horses, localized TBX3 expression changes the distribution of melanocytes and pigment granules within the growing hair, producing radial asymmetry: one side of the hair shaft receives more pigment than the other. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4731265/)

The non-dun alleles do not destroy TBX3 protein function. Instead, they are regulatory mutations that alter where and how much TBX3 is expressed in the hair follicle, resulting in more circumferential pigment distribution and a more saturated, non-diluted coat. [uu](https://www.uu.se/en/news/2015/2015-12-21-genetics-of-camouflage-and-the-dun-pattern-in-horses)

- nd1: a single-nucleotide regulatory change; no dilution, but primitive markings may remain visible. [omia](https://omia.org/OMIA001972/9796/)
- nd2: a 1,617-bp deletion; no dilution and generally no primitive markings. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Evolutionary status
-------------------

Dun is considered the ancestral wild-type state in horses. Ancient DNA from a horse living roughly 43,000 years ago—long before domestication—carried both D and nd1, showing that the dun/non-dun polymorphism predates domestication. [hudsonalpha](https://www.hudsonalpha.org/faculty/greg-barsh/lab/evolution/)

nd2 is inferred to be more recent and likely arose after domestication. Most modern domestic horses are non-dun, which is why intensely pigmented coats are common while dun is comparatively less frequent in many breeds. [hudsonalpha](https://www.hudsonalpha.org/faculty/greg-barsh/lab/evolution/)

Effects outside coat color
--------------------------

There is no robust evidence that the dun locus affects height, speed, athletic ability, muscle mass, bone structure, hoof shape, feathering, or mane/tail length. Its demonstrated effects are confined to hair-follicle pigmentation and the visible coat pattern. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4731265/)

Trait | Effect of dun locus
Red pigment | Diluted on the body; mane/tail and points largely spared  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)
Black pigment | Diluted on the body; mane/tail and points largely spared  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)
Primitive markings | Produced by D; may also occur with nd1  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
Mane/tail color | Not independently changed; they remain the underlying base color’s undiluted or minimally diluted shade  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)
Height, speed, muscle, bone, hooves | No established direct effect  [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4731265/)
Feathering | No established direct effect; feathering is controlled by other loci/polygenic factors
Health | No established dun-associated disorder; the identified variants are regulatory coat-color variants, not known loss-of-function disease mutations  [uu](https://www.uu.se/en/news/2015/2015-12-21-genetics-of-camouflage-and-the-dun-pattern-in-horses)

For a simulator, model dun as a pigment-distribution modifier, not as a structural or performance gene: it changes the visual expression of chestnut, bay, and black bases and adds primitive markings, but should not alter conformation, gait, speed, or health.

Breed distribution
------------------

Dun occurs across many breeds, but it is especially visible where dun is historically common or selected for. UC Davis’s test is breed-general and identifies D, nd1, and nd2 in any horse. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Breeds and populations where dun is commonly seen include:

- Norwegian Fjord — dun is nearly universal and central to the breed’s appearance  
- Icelandic Horse  
- Przewalski’s horse and other wild/feral equid populations  
- Mongolian horses  
- Quarter Horses and related stock-horse breeds  
- Paint Horses and Appaloosas  
- Mustangs  
- Andalusians and Lusitanos  
- Welsh Ponies and Shetland Ponies  
- Morgans, Paso Finos, Peruvian Pasos, and Bashkir Curlies [equinegeneticsresearchcentre](https://equinegeneticsresearchcentre.horse/coat-colour-and-pattern-testing-2/dun-nd1-nd2/)

The frequency differs sharply by breed. In breeds such as the Fjord, dun is a breed-defining trait; in many mainstream sport-horse and Thoroughbred populations, nd2 and non-dun phenotypes are much more prevalent. [omia](https://omia.org/OMIA001972/9796/)

Speculation, controversy, and open questions
--------------------------------------------

The core genetics are settled, but several details remain active research areas:

- Modifier genes for marking intensity. D reliably produces primitive markings, but the strength, number, and placement of stripes vary. Upstream regulators of TBX3 expression are not fully identified. [madbarn](https://madbarn.com/research/differential-expression-and-analysis-of-tbx3-gene-in-skin-tissues-of-dun-mongolian-horses-with-and-without-bider-markings/)
- Bider markings. Recent work on Mongolian horses suggests TBX3 expression may be involved in symmetrical shoulder patches called Bider markings, but the regulatory mechanism remains unresolved. [madbarn](https://madbarn.com/research/differential-expression-and-analysis-of-tbx3-gene-in-skin-tissues-of-dun-mongolian-horses-with-and-without-bider-markings/)
- Zebra-stripe relevance. Researchers have speculated that the developmental signals governing TBX3 expression may help explain stripe formation in zebras, though this remains an evolutionary and developmental-biology question rather than a proven horse-trait mechanism. [uu](https://www.uu.se/en/press/press-releases/2015/2015-12-21-a-horse-of-a-different-colour-genetics-of-camouflage-and-the-dun-pattern)
- Visual misclassification. A dorsal stripe can occur in nd1 horses and can also result from countershading, so phenotype alone cannot reliably distinguish D, nd1, or unrelated marking variation. DNA testing resolves the locus genotype. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
- Possible additional alleles. No additional widely validated alleles at this locus are currently part of standard UC Davis-style dun testing; claims of new “dun types” should be treated cautiously unless supported by published variant discovery, segregation data, and functional evidence. The three-allele model—D, nd1, nd2—is the established framework. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Testing and breeder use
-----------------------

UC Davis’s Veterinary Genetics Laboratory offers a direct Dun Dilution test and includes it in its broader horse coat-color panel. The test distinguishes D, nd1, and nd2, allowing breeders to identify homozygous dun horses and predict whether a non-dun horse can still produce dun offspring. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Practical breeding rules:

- A D/D horse will produce a dun foal with any mate, because every foal inherits D. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
- A D/nd1 or D/nd2 horse has a 50% chance of passing D to each foal. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
- Two non-dun parents can produce a dun foal only if at least one parent carries D; nd1 alone does not create dun dilution. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
- A non-dun horse with primitive markings may be nd1/nd1 or nd1/nd2, not necessarily dun. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)

Simulator implementation notes
------------------------------

For a horse-genetics simulation, represent dun as a three-allele locus with this dominance order:

\[
D > nd1 > nd2
\]

Use these rules:

- D/_ → apply dun dilution to the resolved base coat and enable primitive markings.  
- nd1/nd1 or nd1/nd2 → no dilution, but optionally render variable primitive markings.  
- nd2/nd2 → no dilution and no primitive markings.  
- Do not make D/D visually darker, lighter, or more marked than D/nd1 or D/nd2; current evidence supports no meaningful dosage effect. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/dun-horse)
- Apply dun after resolving MC1R/ASIP base color, but before or alongside independent white-patterning genes depending on how your rendering pipeline prioritizes pattern visibility.  

The most accurate visual outputs are red dun from chestnut, bay/classic dun from bay, and grullo from black. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

The gene, and two different things near it
------------------------------------------

TBX3 is T-box transcription factor 3 - a protein that regulates when and where other genes switch on during development, with broad roles across mammals including limb and skeletal development. What makes it interesting here is that different regulatory variants near the same gene affect two very different traits: the dun coat, and (in certain Chinese horse and pony populations) a substantial component of wither height. The familiar dun test is about the first of those and predicts nothing about the second.

| coat-colour locus | size-associated enhancer
chromosome | ECA8 | ECA8
position | downstream regulatory region of TBX3 | upstream enhancer of TBX3
phenotype | dun dilution and primitive markings | wither height in sampled Chinese horses and ponies
variants | D, nd1, nd2 regulatory haplotypes | TBX3-EN2 A/G at ECA8:20,644,555
same test? | yes - the standard dun test | no - a distinct research marker
health consequence | none recognised | none established

The original mapping put the coat locus in a roughly 200 kb interval on chromosome 8 containing TBX3. On EquCab3 the gene is annotated around 20.51-20.53 Mb; older papers report positions near 18.2 Mb because they used an older assembly. That is an assembly-version mismatch, not two loci. Imsland et al., PMC4731265 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4731265/)

The mechanism, which is inside the hair
---------------------------------------

Dun is not simply less melanin. In a dun horse most body hairs have pigment packed into only about 25-50% of the hair cortex, giving a radially asymmetric, partly diluted shaft. Hairs in the dorsal stripe stay evenly and intensely pigmented, so the stripe stays dark.

The developmental sequence appears to be:

- In a growing follicle, TBX3 is expressed asymmetrically in a subset of hair-bulb keratinocytes.
- That asymmetry changes follicle differentiation and locally reduces expression of KITLG, a major signal supporting melanocyte migration and survival.
- Melanocytes and pigment therefore occupy only part of the developing cortex.
- The finished hair looks diluted, while selected regions keep darker hairs and form the primitive markings.

That explains why dun looks so patterned: it is controlled at a microscopic radial level inside individual hairs as well as by where on the body the follicle sits.

The mod models the outcome, not the hair
One dilution and one mask is a pixel-level restriction plus a marking pass - a per-pixel decision where the biology is a per-hair, sub-hair one. That is the right level for a 64x64 texture, and it is why the mod's markings are drawn as explicit geometry rather than falling out of the dilution the way they do in a real follicle. The one thing the real mechanism predicts and the mod has to assert separately is exactly why the stripe stays dark: in a horse it is the same field, read differently by follicle position.

Three alleles, one dominance order
----------------------------------

D > nd1 > nd2, where D is dun, nd1 is non-dun with possible primitive markings, and nd2 is ordinary non-dun with no genetically expected markings.

allele | molecular description | visible effect
D | ancestral regulatory haplotype, preserving hair-follicle TBX3 regulation | full dun dilution plus primitive markings
nd1 | a regulatory SNP affecting enhancer activity | no body dilution; markings, especially a dorsal stripe, may remain
nd2 | a derived ~1.6 kb deletion in a downstream enhancer | no dilution, no TBX3-caused markings

Some laboratories write d1 and d2 in lower case; same alleles. The mod uses that second convention. A single D is enough to produce dun, so a D/nd2 horse is visibly dun and transmits D to only half its foals, while D/D transmits to all of them.

Two dominance orders over one locus - and the mod says so
The thing this allele series does that most do not is run two orders at once: D alone dilutes, but D and d1 both mark. That is exactly how the mod implements it - two predicates, dilutes() asking for D and marks() asking for D or d1 - and it is a faithful rendering of a real allele series that is genuinely awkward to state in one sentence.

Why a dorsal stripe is not proof of dun
---------------------------------------

A horse can show a countershading stripe, seasonal shading, or an nd1-associated marking without being genetically dun - easily misread in buckskins, bays, red horses and horses carrying other dilutions. Going the other way, the darker the base the harder some markings are to see: in the original work, markings were easier to recognise against red pigment than against dark eumelanin. Phenotype alone misleads; the DNA test is the clean answer.

What it looks like on each base
-------------------------------

base | dun-modified appearance | names
bay | tan or gold body, dark mane, tail and lower legs | bay dun, classic dun, zebra dun
black | slate, smoky or mouse-grey body with dark points | grullo / grulla, blue dun, mouse dun
chestnut | peach, apricot or red-gold body; mane and tail may be darker or more body-coloured | red dun

The most reliable hallmark is the dark dorsal stripe from mane to tail. Others: leg barring (zebra bars), a shoulder cross, dark ear edging, facial masking, cobwebbing or forehead marks, neck and shoulder shadows, dark tail barbs, and dark regions around the muzzle and vibrissae. Expression is variable - not every dun horse shows every marking clearly, which the mod reproduces deliberately in the incomplete-marking rolls.

Red dun: the mod and the source describe different horses
The mod holds KEEP_RED very close to 1 and states outright that a red dun is a chestnut with primitive markings, not a pale horse. The reference here says a chestnut base gives a peach, apricot or red-gold body - a visible dilution - and the cortex mechanism above is pigment-type-agnostic, restricting pigment to 25-50% of the shaft regardless of which melanin is in it. On that reading, red dun should be perceptibly lighter than chestnut.

This is a real disagreement, not a rendering limitation. It is also the one the mod is most likely to be right about for its own purposes: the asymmetry is what makes grullo, bay dun and red dun come out as three visibly different horses from one pair of numbers, and a red dun that is merely "a slightly paler chestnut" is hard to identify in-game. But it should be recorded as a deliberate departure rather than left to be rediscovered, and KEEP_RED is the single number that would move it.

What the coat locus does not do
-------------------------------

There is no good evidence that D/nd1/nd2 changes build, bone, conformation, muscle mass, mature height, frame, movement quality or athletic phenotype. The coat alleles alter a hair-follicle enhancer's activity in a restricted developmental context, and the foundational paper specifically reported no recognised limb-development or other pleiotropic differences between dun and non-dun horses.

So a Fjord's compact sturdy build, a Quarter Horse's muscling and a Konik's hardiness are not consequences of the dun genotype. They are breed-level outcomes from many genes, selection history, management and environment. The mod agrees: dun paints and contributes nothing to the trait layer.

The separate height signal
--------------------------

A different regulatory region upstream of TBX3 has been associated with stature in Chinese horses and ponies. The strongest site, TBX3-EN2, is an A/G SNP at ECA8:20,644,555; in a panel of 763 Chinese horses and ponies the G allele went with greater wither height, and the model estimated it could account for up to about 20.3% of height variation - roughly 10 cm - in that population. PMC8796118 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796118/)

The functional work makes it credible: reporter assays found the G allele increased enhancer activity and TBX3 expression; TBX3 expression was higher in the placentas of larger Yili horses than in Ningqiang ponies; and deleting the corresponding enhancer segment in mice reduced TBX3 expression in developing limbs and produced shorter limb bones. The A allele was common in ponies - 65.6% across seven pony breeds - and fixed in the 13 sampled Przewalski's horses; G averaged 81.2% across 18 Chinese horse breeds, ranging from 21.1% in Debao ponies to 85.7% in Yili horses.

This is not the dun test, and the mod does not model it
The coat variants and TBX3-EN2 are separate regulatory changes in different regions around the same gene. A dun horse can be small or large; a non-dun horse can be small or large. Height is polygenic and environmentally influenced, and the EN2 result - powerful in the Chinese populations studied - is not a universal one-marker height calculator: Western pony breeds show different major signals, HMGA2 in Falabellas among them, so similar stature arises by different genetic routes. The mod models the coat locus only, and puts size on LCORL and HMGA2. That is the correct split.

Health, jumping, speed
----------------------

Dun is a pigmentation trait, not a disorder. OMIA classifies it as not disease-related, and the foundational study found no developmental, limb or systemic effects attributable to the coat variants.

That needs distinguishing from the wider biology of the gene. In humans, damaging one copy of TBX3 causes ulnar-mammary syndrome - limb, tooth, gland and genital abnormalities. That does not mean a dun horse carries a disease mutation: the equine coat variants leave the protein intact and modify hair-follicle expression rather than removing the gene's developmental function. It is the cleanest example on this wiki of why "this gene causes X in humans" is not an argument about a horse.

For the size enhancer there is likewise no established equine health syndrome or soundness risk. The mouse enhancer-deletion experiments show why TBX3 can affect limb growth; they are not evidence that naturally occurring horse genotypes cause orthopaedic disease, hoof disease, infertility, metabolic disease or reduced lifespan.

Jumping. No validated evidence that any of these variants predicts scope, technique, carefulness, bascule, soundness under jumping load or competition success. TBX3's developmental role makes it tempting to speculate about limb architecture and biomechanics; that inference has not been demonstrated in horses, and would need replicated genotype-phenotype associations controlling for breed, height, training, rider, pedigree, competition level and other athletic loci.

Speed. No robust evidence that the dun alleles affect sprint speed, stamina, earnings, stride mechanics or gait. The speed-gene discussion centres on MSTN. The Chinese size variant could in theory correlate indirectly with body dimensions where it affects height - which is not causing speed, and taller is not inherently faster.

Breeds and frequency
--------------------

The coat work genotyped 1,814 animals from more than 45 breeds, confirming that TBX3 variation is broad rather than confined to one "primitive" breed. Breed-by-breed frequencies remain unevenly measured, and many figures circulating online rest on phenotypes, registry preferences or small samples rather than population surveys.

OMIA documents the dun phenotype or the causal variants in the American Trotter, Arabian, American Bashkir Curly, Faroes Pony, Norwegian Fjord, Gotland Pony, Icelandic Horse, Mongolian Horse, Polish Konik, Quarter Horse, Shetland Pony, Swedish Warmblood and Vyatka. Variant-specific reporting is narrower:

- D in Curly, Fjord, Icelandic, Polish Konik, Quarter Horse, Vyatka and the Bosnian Mountain Horse.
- nd1 in American Trotter, Arabian, Curly, Faroes Pony, Gotland Pony, Mongolian, Quarter Horse, Shetland Pony, Swedish Warmblood and Vyatka.
- nd2 is the reference-genome allele, widespread, and the most prevalent allele among common domestic breeds.

population | best-supported conclusion
Norwegian Fjord | D commonly described as fixed or effectively so; dun is a defining breed feature, though base colour still modifies how it presents
Polish Konik | common and a direct selection target; genomic scans show a strong selection signal at or near TBX3 in blue-dun Koniks
Icelandic Horse | D well established and historically used in inheritance studies; no modern population-wide frequency in the sources here
Quarter Horse | D exists and produces recognisable duns; not universal, and no representative contemporary survey
Mongolian Horse | dun and nd1 documented; phenotype and local structure vary enough that one breed-wide percentage would overstate the data
most modern breeds | nd2 is the most prevalent allele
Przewalski's horse | dun is characteristic; all sampled animals in the size study carried the ancestral EN2 A allele
Bosnian Mountain Horse | the one real breed-wide D figure on this page: 0.09, genotyped in 313 animals from the breeders' association records. The same study classified only ~8% of the sample as dun by eye, where an allele frequency of 0.09 predicts about 17% showing it - the authors report the genotype/phenotype discrepancy themselves. Its breeding programme excludes grey, pinto, chestnut and spotted, so dun is the only dilution left to see

The distinction between "known to occur" and "common" matters here more than anywhere. For Arabians, Swedish Warmbloods, American Trotters and Shetlands, documentation of nd1 does not mean many individuals are visibly dun - nd1 is a non-dun allele that merely preserves subtle markings.

The mod uses D at 1/24 (4.2%) and d1 at 1/10 (10%), so a founder is more than twice as likely to carry the marking allele as the dilution - which matches the qualitative picture above, where nd1 turns up in far more breeds than D does. No published global figure exists to check either number against.

One breed here is pinned to a measured number instead. The Domestic Mountain Pony takes D straight from the Bosnian figure above, which makes it the only breed in the mod whose dun rate is a published allele frequency rather than a design call - and it inherits that study's disagreement with itself, since the file follows the allele frequency and so runs about twice as many duns as the same study counted by eye. That was a deliberate choice of the genotype over the eyeball, and it is the one to revisit if duns look too common in play.

Evolution, and the open questions
---------------------------------

Well established. Dun is likely the ancestral equid coat state, seen in Przewalski's horses and other wild equids, and plausibly adaptive camouflage - inferred from ecology and phylogenetic distribution rather than directly quantified. The D and nd1 haplotypes predate domestication: ancient DNA identified a roughly 42,700-year-old horse carrying D/nd1 and a roughly 4,400-year-old horse inferred nd1/nd1. nd2 is more recent, was not observed outside domestic horses in that study, and likely rose during domestication alongside selection for more intensely pigmented, non-camouflage colours. And all three are regulatory changes - they alter where TBX3 is expressed in a growing follicle, not the protein's amino-acid sequence.

Speculative or incomplete. The camouflage advantage is plausible and often stated, and its magnitude and historical setting are unresolved. Researchers have proposed that spatial TBX3 regulation could illuminate the evolution of zebra stripes - TBX3 is not established as the zebra-stripe gene, which is worth knowing next to the mod's own zebra genes. Work in dun Mongolian horses is exploring differential TBX3 skin expression in relation to Bider markings, an active area that does not change the central point: dun expression is patterned and region-specific rather than on/off. Claims that dun changes temperament, durability, athleticism or "primitive hardiness" are unsupported unless they can separate TBX3 from breed ancestry and selection. And the TBX3-EN2 height finding needs validation outside the Chinese populations before it can be a general stature predictor.
