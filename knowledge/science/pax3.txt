This settles what SW9 and SW10 are. No address has been opened by a maintainer. Her
answer, condensed, with every point and address she gave (all from the paper,
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37406837/, unless marked):

  - SW9 and SW10 are two separate splash-white alleles found in Pura Raza Espanola
    (PRE, Andalusian) horses in 2023: SW9 a missense variant in MITF, SW10 a stop-gain
    (nonsense) variant in PAX3. Different genes, different chromosomes; a horse can
    carry one, the other, both or neither.
  - The paper: McFadden A, Martin K, Foster G, Vierra M, Lundquist EW, Everts RE,
    Martin E, Volz E, McLoone K, Brooks SA, Lafayette C (2023). "Two Novel Variants in
    MITF and PAX3 Associated With Splashed White Phenotypes in Horses." Journal of
    Equine Veterinary Science 128:104875. doi:10.1016/j.jevs.2023.104875. The authors
    proposed the names SW9 (MITF) and SW10 (PAX3).
  - SW9: MITF, NM_001163874.1:c.993A>T, missense in a MITF binding domain,
    ECA16:21,559,940 A>T on EquCab3.0. Splashed white markings and blue eyes; mean
    white score 24.45.
  - SW10: PAX3, XM_005610643.3:c.927C>T, stop-gain, early truncation,
    ECA6:11,196,181 C>T on EquCab3.0. Splashed white markings and blue eyes; mean white
    score 25.50.
  - Association in PRE horses: PAX3 variant P = 1.144e-11 (N = 30), MITF variant
    P = 4.441e-16 (N = 39); controls averaged a white score of 1.89. Functional
    predictions (I-TASSER, SMART, VEP, SIFT) moderate to extreme. The evidence is
    mainly association, not functional proof.
  - Both act on pigment cells: they leave white areas and do not change red or black
    pigment, agouti or extension. Blue eyes were part of the association for both.
    Deafness is a plausible concern for severe MITF/PAX3 white generally but is not
    shown for SW9 or SW10.
  - SW9/SW9 and SW10/SW10: no validated inheritance or viability data. SW10 truncates
    PAX3 before an important DNA-binding domain, and later reviews say it is predicted
    to be embryonic lethal in homozygotes: a prediction, not a demonstrated outcome.
  - A horse with SW9 and SW10 carries two independent splash variants and may be whiter
    than with either alone; not characterised.
  - Treat both as PRE-associated until other surveys find them elsewhere. UC Davis groups
    SW1, SW3 and SW5-SW8 under MITF and SW2 and SW4 under PAX3, and its page names only
    SW1-SW8 (https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white). European laboratories such
    as Genomia list SW9 and SW10; the paper is the primary source for them.
  - No good evidence of any effect on height, speed, muscle, bone, hooves, feathering,
    or mane and tail colour beyond white where the area is depigmented.

What the gene files do with it: SW9 (mitf) and SW10 (pax3) are no longer "unconfirmed".
The mod had put each in the right gene. One copy gives the ordinary splash outcome;
two copies are "never detected"; a pair with another variant of the same gene is
undocumented. The "unconfirmed-allele" outcome is gone.

FIRST ANSWER: the PAX3 locus in horses
--------------------------------------

PAX3 (Paired Box 3) is a transcription-factor gene on horse chromosome 6 (ECA6) that regulates development and survival of neural-crest-derived pigment cells. In horses, its confirmed coat-color role is splashed white spotting, not a change in the fundamental red/black pigment chemistry. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

Established facts
-----------------

Item | Established finding
Gene | PAX3 (paired box gene 3), a developmental transcription factor.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
Chromosomal location | ECA6; the original SW2 variant was mapped to ECA6:g.11,429,753C>T (EquCab2.0).  [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)
Biological role | Required for normal development of some nerve tissue and pigment cells; PAX3 also helps regulate MITF, a central melanocyte gene.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
Confirmed horse alleles | SW2 (PAX3 p.Cys70Tyr / c.209G>A) and SW4 (PAX3 p.Pro32Arg).  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
Inheritance | Autosomal dominant with variable expression; one copy can produce splashed white.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
Main phenotype | Broad blaze/bald face, high white leg markings, variable belly white, and often blue or partially blue eyes.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
Health association | Deafness can occur in some splashed white horses; it is variable and not every SW horse is deaf.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)

Alleles at PAX3
---------------

Allele | Molecular change | Known expression | Homozygote status
N | Normal PAX3 | No PAX3-associated splashed white | Normal
SW2 | Missense p.Cys70Tyr; affects the conserved paired DNA-binding domain | Splashed white, highly variable; originally traced to a Quarter Horse founder line | Viable; UC Davis reports identified SW2/SW2 horses, though they may be deaf and warrant veterinary evaluation.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
SW4 | Missense p.Pro32Arg | Splashed white or, at minimum, a broad blaze; rare Appaloosa-family variant | Possibly embryonic lethal, but this remains unproven because no SW4/SW4 horse has been reported.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)

UC Davis currently recognizes SW2 and SW4 as the PAX3 splashed-white alleles; SW1, SW3, SW5, SW6, SW7, and SW8 are MITF variants. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)

Effect on red and black pigment
-------------------------------

PAX3 does not determine whether a horse makes red versus black pigment. That baseline is governed primarily by MC1R/Extension for black pigment and ASIP/Agouti for its distribution; PAX3 acts upstream during melanocyte development. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

What PAX3 variants do is reduce or prevent pigment cells from reaching or surviving in particular skin regions. The affected areas therefore produce white hair over pink skin, regardless of whether the horse’s genotype would otherwise have produced chestnut, bay, or black pigment. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

- A chestnut SW2 horse remains genetically red-based; its body hairs are still red where pigment cells are present.
- A bay or black SW2 horse still produces black-based pigment in pigmented regions.
- The visible “splash” is a localized absence of melanocytes, not dilution of eumelanin or pheomelanin. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

One modifier effect is well supported: chestnut horses carrying a PAX3 and/or MITF splash allele tend to show more facial white than bay horses with the same splash genotype, so base color modifies how obvious the pattern appears. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

What PAX3 does not do
---------------------

There is no good evidence that PAX3 variants affect:

- Height or body size
- Speed or athletic performance
- Muscle mass or muscle fiber type
- Bone structure
- Hoof shape or hoof quality
- Feathering
- Mane or tail color, except that white spotting can make mane/tail hair white where the underlying skin lacks melanocytes
- Temperament

PAX3 is developmentally important, and human PAX3 mutations can involve craniofacial and hearing traits, but the equine evidence specifically connects the known horse variants to pigmentation and variable deafness, not conformation or performance traits. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

Breeds and frequency
--------------------

Allele | Breeds where documented
SW2 | Certain Quarter Horse and Paint Horse lines; also reported in Noriker and Lipizzaner horses in broader research.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
SW4 | A rare variant identified in an Appaloosa family.  [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)

SW2 is low-frequency even within Quarter Horses and Paint Horses: a multi-breed survey found it only in Paint Horses and Quarter Horses, with allele frequencies of about 0.0078 and 0.0083 respectively after removing related individuals. [escholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4tv6j5qn/qt4tv6j5qn.pdf)

Because Quarter Horses and Paint Horses share substantial ancestry and some horses are double-registered, SW2 is best understood as a Quarter Horse/Paint Horse lineage variant rather than a breed-exclusive mutation. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

Speculation and controversy
---------------------------

- SW4 homozygote viability: UC Davis says SW4/SW4 “may” be embryonic lethal but has not been demonstrated; no living homozygote has been identified. This should be treated as a precaution, not a proven lethal. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- Older SW2 lethality claim: The original 2012 paper predicted that SW2/SW2 might be nonviable based on mouse biology, but UC Davis later reported identified SW2 homozygotes, so the current position is that SW2 homozygosity is likely viable. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)
- Deafness risk: Deafness is real but incompletely penetrant and variable. It is not safe to assume every SW2 horse is deaf, nor that a minimally marked SW horse cannot have hearing deficits. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)
- Unexplained splash phenotypes: A horse can look splashed white yet test N/N for all currently known SW alleles; UC Davis explicitly notes that other white-spotting mutations or undiscovered variants may be responsible. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- “SW10” claims: Some third-party sources mention additional PAX3-associated variants, but they are not part of UC Davis’s current SW1–SW8 testing framework. For breeding decisions, rely on laboratory-validated allele names and tests rather than informal allele numbering. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)

Future science
--------------

Likely future work includes:

- Finding additional white-spotting variants in horses whose splash-like phenotype remains genetically unexplained. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- Quantifying how modifier genes, base color, and other white-pattern alleles shape the amount and placement of white. UC Davis notes that much of this variation is still unexplained. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- Clarifying SW4 homozygote viability and the precise genotype combinations that raise deafness risk. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- Better defining PAX3’s regulatory relationship with MITF and other neural-crest genes in horses. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

Practical breeding notes
------------------------

- N/SW2 × N/N: 50% of foals expected to inherit SW2.
- N/SW2 × N/SW2: 25% SW2/SW2, 50% N/SW2, 25% N/N; because homozygous SW2 horses may be deaf, clinical hearing evaluation is prudent. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- SW4 carriers: Avoid pairing two SW4 carriers until homozygote viability is resolved. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)
- Combining splash alleles: A horse can carry a PAX3 allele plus an MITF allele; combinations can produce much more white or an entirely white coat. UC Davis specifically lists SW2/SW4 as a combination not yet detected and of unknown viability. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/splashed-white)

For a simulation, model PAX3 as a dominant white-spotting locus with variable expression, separate from base-color genes, with SW2 viable but associated with a deafness risk flag and SW4 flagged as potentially homozygous-lethal pending better data. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

SECOND ANSWER: is PAX3 behind ordinary markings?
------------------------------------------------

No—PAX3 is not the main gene behind ordinary blazes, stars, stripes, socks, and stockings. Those common markings are best understood as polygenic and incompletely mapped, with KIT and related melanocyte-development pathways contributing much more broadly than PAX3. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871141319313125)

What controls ordinary markings
-------------------------------

Ordinary white markings arise when melanocytes fail to populate, survive in, or produce pigment in particular patches of skin during embryonic development. The tendency is heritable, but the exact placement and extent of a blaze or socks are not currently predictable from a single gene test. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871141319313125)

Marking type | Best current genetic explanation
Star, stripe, snip, blaze, socks, stockings | Polygenic; no single “ordinary markings” gene has been identified.  [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871141319313125)
Larger white patterns, sabino-like white, roan, dominant white | Often associated with variants in KIT or its regulatory region.  [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0737080623003660)
Splashed white patterns | Known variants occur in MITF and PAX3.  [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)
Frame overo | EDNRB.  [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)
Leopard complex patterns | TRPM1.  [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

Why PAX3 is different
---------------------

PAX3 variants cause a recognizable splashed white phenotype—often a broad or bald face, extended leg white, belly white, and blue eyes—not the everyday random markings seen on most horses. 

A horse can have a huge blaze and four white socks while testing N/N for SW2 and SW4, the two known PAX3 splashed-white alleles. Conversely, an SW2 carrier may have relatively little white. That variability is why PAX3 should not be treated as the “markings gene.” [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854588/)

KIT is closer, but still not the whole answer
---------------------------------------------

KIT is the strongest known candidate system for white patterning because it affects melanocyte survival, proliferation, and migration. More than 30–35 KIT variants have been associated with equine white spotting, ranging from subtle increases in facial/leg white to extensive body white or complete white coats. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854588/)

However, even KIT does not explain every ordinary marking. Genome-wide studies in Quarter Horses found genetic regions associated with head and leg markings, but did not identify one master locus that predicts individual blaze or sock patterns.  A KIT variant can raise the likelihood of white, while base color and developmental chance influence how much white ultimately appears. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871141319313125)

Practical model for a simulator
-------------------------------

For a horse-breeding simulator, I’d separate these systems:

- Base color: MC1R and ASIP determine red/black pigment and its distribution. 
- Common markings: A polygenic “white-marking score” with random developmental variation; do not tie it to PAX3.
- Named white patterns: Separate loci/alleles for KIT-related sabino/dominant white, MITF/PAX3 splashed white, EDNRB frame overo, and TRPM1 leopard complex. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)
- PAX3 specifically: SW2 and SW4 as dominant splashed-white alleles with variable expression, not general blaze/sock modifiers. 

So: PAX3 affects a particular type of white spotting, while the ordinary white markings most horses have are a separate, largely unresolved polygenic trait. [sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871141319313125)

At a glance
-----------

Gene | PAX3 - paired box 3, a developmental transcription factor
Chromosome | Equine chromosome 6 (ECA6)
Test name | Splash White 2, SW2
Variant | PAX3:c.209G>A, p.Cys70Tyr (p.C70Y); EquCab2 ECA6:g.11,429,753C>T
Inheritance | Autosomal dominant, with variable expression and incomplete penetrance at the visible-coat level
Heterozygote | May show little white or classic splash; may have one or two blue eyes; may be congenitally deaf
Homozygote | Viable - not an established embryonic lethal - usually much more extensively white, and may be deaf
Main health concern | Congenital sensorineural deafness in some, not all, affected horses
Breeds | American Quarter Horse and American Paint Horse

What PAX3 does
--------------

PAX3 encodes a transcription factor - a protein that binds DNA and regulates other genes during embryonic development. Its relevant roles are in neural-crest development; the migration and survival of melanoblasts, the embryonic precursors of melanocytes; some nervous-system and muscle lineages; and the activation of pigment pathways including regulation connected to MITF.

Melanocytes migrate from the neural crest to skin, hair follicles, eyes and inner ear. Where fewer precursors arrive, or fewer survive, that tissue has no pigment: white hair and pink skin in the coat, blue or partly blue eyes in the iris, and a predisposition to congenital deafness in the inner ear. That is one mechanism producing three apparently unrelated symptoms - and it is why the mod's eye-colour hooks and its deafness condition both hang off the white-pattern genes rather than off anything of their own. Hauswirth et al., PMC3325211 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3325211/)

SW2 does not switch PAX3 off. One copy creates a partial, dosage-sensitive developmental effect, and the visible result is a patchy absence of pigment cells rather than a whole-body one.

The mutation
------------

The substitution sits in the paired domain, one of PAX3's key DNA-binding domains. The cysteine at position 70 is highly conserved across PAX-family proteins and across animals, and structural comparison with human PAX6 suggests it normally helps contact the DNA backbone - so replacing it with tyrosine should alter DNA binding and downstream regulation.

The case for causality is strong, and much of the mechanism is inferred rather than measured in equine embryonic tissue: linkage in a Quarter Horse family pointed to ECA6, where PAX3 sits; the allele was found in splash-white horses and not in the solid-coloured controls examined; PAX3's biological role fits abnormal pigment-cell development directly; comparable variants in humans and mice cause pigmentary and hearing phenotypes; and the affected residue is conserved and functionally crucial.

Coat and eye
------------

SW2 is a white spotting pattern, not a dilution. The base colour still exists in the pigmented areas; SW2 removes pigment where melanocytes are missing. Typical features: a large broad blaze; a bald face, often over or past the eyes and onto the cheeks; white lower legs, sometimes carried high; one or two blue eyes, or partial blue and sectoral heterochromia; small belly spots or more extensive body white; and the impression of a horse dipped in white from below.

Expression is highly variable. One N/SW2 horse has a bald face and four high whites; another has ordinary socks and a modest blaze. Visual identification is unreliable. Horses carrying SW2 and SW1 averaged more facial depigmentation than horses carrying either alone, and chestnut horses in that study showed more extensive white face than bays of the same splash genotype - so base colour and other modifiers matter.

visible white = SW2 effect + other white-pattern alleles + base colour and modifiers + developmental variation

So markings cannot tell you whether a horse has SW2, or whether it has one copy or two.

Deafness
--------

The well-supported health association is congenital sensorineural deafness. Some splash-white horses are deaf; many are not, and the research and laboratory guidance do not support treating it as inevitable.

The mechanism is developmental. Melanocytes matter to normal inner-ear physiology: in the cochlea, melanocyte-like cells in the stria vascularis help maintain the ionic environment hearing depends on. If the pigment pathway leaves the inner ear without enough functional melanocytes, the hearing loss is permanent.

- It is usually present from birth, not progressive.
- A blue eye is a clue to altered pigment-cell distribution and is not a hearing test.
- Extensive facial white, particularly around the ears, raises suspicion; appearance cannot diagnose it.
- BAER testing is the objective method where hearing matters for safety, training, breeding or sale disclosure.
- A deaf horse can live and work successfully with predictable routines, visual cues and safety-aware handling.

The mod makes deafness a rule where reality makes it a risk
In the mod, two variant copies at this locus and the horse is deaf - deterministically, and one copy never is. Reality is messier in both directions: deafness has incomplete penetrance even in homozygotes, and it does occur in some heterozygous splash-white horses. The exact penetrance for N/SW2 and SW2/SW2 is listed among the open questions below.

The mod's version is the legible one: a player can predict it, breed around it, and learn the rule from one affected horse. A probabilistic condition on a locus that already has variable coat expression would be indistinguishable from noise. It is the same trade GYS1 makes with penetrance, and it is worth recording as a simplification rather than a finding.

What SW2 is not known to cause
------------------------------

trait | evidence
height and mature size | no validated association
conformation - skeleton, balance, bone, topline, limb angle, muscling | none established
foal growth and adult development | no evidence of impairment
speed, racing, stride mechanics, aerobic capacity | no published evidence
endurance, cardiovascular fitness, metabolism, recovery | none established
jumping scope, technique, bascule, carefulness, coordination, soundness | no evidence
temperament and trainability | no direct effect; a deaf horse responds differently because it cannot use sound cues
eye disease merely from a blue eye | no evidence
seizures, neurologic disease, immune disorder, heterozygote infertility | no evidence

The caveat that matters: deafness changes management, not inherent athletic ability. A deaf horse may need training adapted to vibration, body language, touch, visual signals and an awareness of startling risk. No study establishes an SW2-specific reduction in athletic capacity. People infer physical traits from the striking look of a high-white horse; there is no basis for it. SW2 affects embryonic patterning of melanocytes and is not a quantitative trait locus for conformation or sport.

Inheritance
-----------

mating | expected per foal
N/N x N/N | 100% N/N
N/SW2 x N/N | 50% / 50%
N/SW2 x N/SW2 | 25 / 50 / 25
SW2/SW2 x N/N | 100% N/SW2
SW2/SW2 x N/SW2 | 50% / 50%
SW2/SW2 x SW2/SW2 | 100% SW2/SW2

Dominant genetically; variable in expression. A horse can inherit SW2 and show only modest markings. So SW2 is dominant with variable expressivity, not "one allele equals a fixed paint pattern". Other loci add or alter white: SW1 and the other MITF splash alleles, tobiano, frame overo, sabino 1 and the other KIT variants, W20 and the dominant whites, and the base colour itself.

The homozygote, and a hypothesis that did not survive
-----------------------------------------------------

Early work found no SW2/SW2 horses. Because PAX3 matters in neural development, the 2012 discovery paper reasonably raised the possibility that homozygosity might be embryonic or fetal lethal, partly on mouse biology. That was a hypothesis.

Large-scale genotyping settled it. A UC Davis dataset of 11,281 horses found nine SW2/SW2 animals - three Paints and six Quarter Horses. So the genotype is not universally lethal. The six homozygous Quarter Horses with photographs were all described as all-white, and five also carried one or more additional white-patterning alleles. PMC9498372 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9498372/)

Careful conclusion: homozygotes can be born and survive; they tend toward far more extensive depigmentation than heterozygotes; the data are too limited to predict a uniform SW2/SW2 pattern; many known examples have confounding stacked white alleles; and the frequency and severity of deafness among them is not well quantified. UC Davis describes homozygosity as likely non-lethal while noting such horses may be deaf and warrant veterinary evaluation.

The mod tracks this correctly, including the negative case
SW2/SW2 is viable in the mod and lands on the bold-splash outcome, which matches the post-2022 picture rather than the superseded lethality hypothesis. And SW4/SW4 is excluded from the catalogue by canOccur because it has never been detected in a live horse - a rarer and more careful distinction than most of the mod makes, and the same mechanism MET uses for its own reason.

Breeds and frequency
--------------------

In the 11,281-horse, 28-breed study, SW2 was found in Quarter Horses and Paint Horses only.

breed | sampled | N/SW2 | SW2/SW2 | filtered n | allele frequency
American Paint Horse | 2,080 | 63 | 3 | 1,020 | 0.78%
American Quarter Horse | 5,518 | 202 | 6 | 3,193 | 0.83%

That is an allele frequency near 0.8%, not a carrier percentage. For a rare dominant allele, heterozygotes are about 2p(1-p), so 0.008 corresponds to roughly 1.5-1.7% heterozygotes under random mating, before selection and relatedness. Quarter Horse and Paint registries have historical gene flow and dual registration, so the two figures are not independent breed estimates, and the original research treated them as one connected population.

The mod carries WILD_SW2_PERCENT = 3.0 and WILD_SW4_PERCENT = 0.5 - SW2 roughly twice the real stock-horse heterozygote rate, and SW4 rarer still, which is right: SW4 is one Appaloosa family and nothing else.

SW2 is the rare one, and it is rare because it is new
Genetic evidence suggests SW2 began as a recent de novo germline mutation in a Quarter Horse mare born in 1987. Hair-root DNA from that mare tested wild type while two splash-white sons carried SW2 - so the mutation most likely arose in the germ cells that produced her offspring rather than throughout her sampled tissue. That is the whole reason for the asymmetry the mod encodes: SW1 on MITF is the widespread, several-centuries-old, minimally expressed allele, and SW2 is a coding change in PAX3 that is barely forty years old and concentrated in one stock-horse family.

SW2 against the rest of the splash family
-----------------------------------------

"Splash white" is a phenotype category, not a gene. At least two genes produce splash-like patterning:

allele | gene | distinction
SW1 | MITF | broadly distributed across breeds; a regulatory / promoter variant
SW2 | PAX3 | the p.Cys70Tyr missense; strongest evidence in Quarter Horse and Paint lines
SW3 | MITF | rare frameshift allele in Quarter Horse and Paint lines
SW4 | PAX3 | rare, documented in Appaloosa-family material
SW5-SW8 | MITF | rare, many lineage- or breed-restricted
SW10 | PAX3 | a separate stop-gain allele in Pura Raza Española horses - not SW2

So a horse can look splashy and test N/N for SW2 because it has SW1, a different splash allele, a KIT white allele, frame overo, an untested variant, or a combination - and an SW2-positive horse can look only subtly marked. The mod carries SW2 and SW4 on this locus and SW1 on its MITF twin, which is the correct split: they are different chromosomes' worth of gene, so a horse can be splash twice over, and one carrying both is whiter than either alone with no special case anywhere.

What is settled, and what is not
--------------------------------

Well supported. SW2 is the PAX3 p.Cys70Tyr variant on ECA6, inherited as an autosomal dominant white-pattern allele; it affects melanocyte development and produces variable splash-white spotting with broad facial white, leg white, blue or partly blue eyes and sometimes extensive body white; some splash-white horses have congenital deafness; SW2 interacts with SW1 to increase average white; homozygotes exist, so the genotype is not universally lethal; and the documented distribution is Quarter Horse and Paint.

Still uncertain. The exact molecular change in DNA binding and downstream expression caused by p.C70Y in living equine pigment cells; the exact penetrance of deafness for heterozygotes and homozygotes; whether SW2 alone accounts for the all-white homozygote phenotypes, given that most known homozygotes carry additional white alleles; the precise influence of base colour, MITF and KIT alleles and unknown modifiers; and the real frequency in individual sublines, regions and programmes.

Not supported. SW2 as a speed, racing, jumping, endurance or conformation gene; that it improves or impairs athletic potential; that a blue eye proves deafness; that an SW2-positive horse is automatically deaf; that a splashy horse is automatically SW2-positive; or that homozygous SW2 is categorically lethal - that concern was superseded by living homozygotes.
