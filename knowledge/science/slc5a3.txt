SLC5A3 is not a coat-color locus in horses. It is best known in the horse as the gene containing the mutation that causes Foal Immunodeficiency Syndrome (FIS), a fatal autosomal-recessive immune and blood disorder primarily associated with Fell and Dales Ponies. [25][24]

Locus overview
--------------

Item | Established information
Gene symbol | SLC5A3
Protein | Sodium/myo-inositol cotransporter, also called SMIT or SMIT1
Function | Transports myo-inositol across the cell membrane together with sodium ions; important for osmotic regulation and cellular inositol supply. [24][5]
Horse chromosome | ECA26
Reported critical region | Approximately 29.8–30.8 Mb on ECA26; the FIS-associated SNP was mapped at ECA26:30,660,224 in the original study. [24]
FIS variant | c.1337G>T, producing p.P446L — proline replaced by leucine at amino-acid position 446. [25][24]
Inheritance | Autosomal recessive. [25]
Main affected breeds | Fell Pony and Dales Pony, including crosses. [25][31]
UC Davis test alleles | N = normal; FIS = FIS-associated variant. [25]

What SLC5A3 does
----------------

SLC5A3 encodes a membrane transporter that cotransports sodium and myo-inositol. Myo-inositol is an osmolyte and signaling-related molecule, and the transporter helps cells maintain volume and fluid balance under osmotic stress, particularly in tissues such as the nervous system, kidney, and fetoplacental unit. [5][24]

In the original FIS study, the equine variant was a single-nucleotide change within the single exon of SLC5A3. It changes a highly conserved proline at position 446 to leucine, and the authors proposed that this alters transporter function. [24]

Alleles in horses
-----------------

UC Davis recognizes two reportable alleles for the FIS test:

Genotype | Interpretation | Breeding outcome
N/N | No FIS variant detected. | Does not produce FIS-affected foals with any mate. [25]
N/FIS | Carrier; clinically normal. | Can pass FIS to about 50% of foals; mating two carriers gives a 25% chance of an affected foal. [25]
FIS/FIS | Affected. | Develops FIS, which is invariably fatal. [25]

There is currently no well-established third allele at the equine SLC5A3 FIS test locus. The validated clinical test distinguishes normal from the FIS-associated variant; it is not a documented coat-color, dilution, or performance allele system. [25]

Effect on red and black pigment
-------------------------------

SLC5A3 does not control red versus black pigment. The core equine pigment switches are:

- MC1R / Extension (E): determines whether black pigment can be produced; recessive e/e horses are chestnut/red-based. [20][21]
- ASIP / Agouti (A): in an E-based horse, determines whether black is restricted to points, producing bay, or distributed over the body, producing black. [20][21]

Thus, a Fell or Dales Pony’s FIS genotype has no direct expected effect on whether it is chestnut, bay, black, palomino, buckskin, gray, or any other coat color. FIS is a health/immune-system locus, not a pigment locus. [25][20]

Clinical effects: FIS
---------------------

Foals with FIS/FIS may look normal at birth, but within weeks develop severe, progressive anemia and immunodeficiency. They have low red blood cells and impaired B-lymphocyte function, leading to poor antibody production and vulnerability to infections such as diarrhea and pneumonia. [24][31]

Affected foals do not respond effectively to treatment and die or are euthanized, typically before three months of age in the original description. [24] UC Davis describes FIS as invariably fatal and notes that there is no effective treatment. [25][31]

Breeds and carrier history
--------------------------

FIS was identified in the Fell Pony and subsequently in the related Dales Pony, both rare native British pony breeds. [24][25]

In the 2011 population screen reported with the gene discovery:

Breed | Carriers identified | Sample size | Carrier frequency
Fell Pony | 82 | 214 | 38% [24]
Dales Pony | 16 | 87 | 18% [24]

Those carrier frequencies were consistent with reported disease prevalence of roughly 10% in Fell foals and 1% in Dales foals at the time. [24][25] Because affected foals do not survive to breeding age, affected foals arise specifically from carrier-to-carrier matings. [25]

Speculation, controversy, and uncertainty
-----------------------------------------

The association between the SLC5A3 p.P446L variant and FIS is extremely strong: all available affected foals in the discovery study were homozygous for the variant, and obligate carriers were heterozygous. [24] UC Davis likewise states that the SNP was perfectly correlated with FIS. [25]

The main uncertainty is mechanistic, not whether the variant is associated with disease. UC Davis notes that exactly how the amino-acid substitution produces the immune and erythroid phenotypes is not yet fully understood; it may involve partial loss of function or altered function in osmotic-stress response. [25]

Reasonable open questions include:

- Whether p.P446L reduces myo-inositol transport, changes transporter regulation, or has tissue-specific effects.
- Why the defect particularly compromises B lymphocytes and red-blood-cell production.
- Whether modifier genes, developmental timing, or environmental infection pressure influence the severity or timing of disease.
- Whether other rare SLC5A3 variants exist in horses, and whether any have functional effects.

Those are research questions, not established allele categories for breeding or simulation purposes. [25][24]

What it does not establish
--------------------------

There is no credible evidence that the horse FIS variant affects:

- Base coat color, red/black pigment switching, or dilution.
- Mane or tail color.
- Height, speed, athletic performance, muscle mass, or bone structure.
- Hoof shape or feathering.
- White patterning or marking genetics.

Those traits are governed by other loci or polygenic architecture. For example, equine base color depends on MC1R and ASIP, while cream and pearl are alleles of SLC45A2. [20][21] Any simulator should therefore treat SLC5A3 as a health/lethal recessive trait, not as a visual coat trait.

Future science
--------------

Future work could directly measure the transport activity of normal versus p.P446L equine SLC5A3, characterize inositol levels and osmotic stress responses in affected tissues, and investigate why the mutation causes B-cell and erythroid failure. [25][24]

For breed conservation, the practical science is already clear: DNA-test breeding stock, avoid pairing two carriers, and use carrier-to-normal matings where appropriate to reduce variant frequency while preserving genetic diversity. UC Davis specifically recommends avoiding carrier-to-carrier matings and notes that carrier-to-N/N or N/N-to-N/N matings will not produce affected foals. [25]

Simulator implementation note
-----------------------------

For a horse-genetics simulator, model SLC5A3 as:

- Locus name: FIS / SLC5A3
- Alleles: N, FIS
- Inheritance: autosomal recessive
- Phenotype:
  - N/N: healthy
  - N/FIS: healthy carrier
  - FIS/FIS: fatal FIS phenotype
- Visual expression: none
- Breed weighting: elevated allele frequency in Fell Pony and Dales Pony populations; low or absent elsewhere unless explicitly introduced through ancestry. [25][24]

EARLIER NOTES, FROM THE MOD'S WIKI

At a glance
-----------

Gene | SLC5A3 - solute carrier family 5 member 3, a sodium/myo-inositol cotransporter
Disorder | Foal immunodeficiency syndrome (FIS); formerly "Fell Pony syndrome"
Inheritance | Autosomal recessive
Homozygote | B-lymphocyte failure and progressive anaemia; invariably fatal in the first months
Heterozygote | Clinically normal carrier
Coat | No known effect

What breaks
-----------

Two things fail together, which is what made the condition confusing before the causal variant was found. Affected foals cannot maintain a B-lymphocyte population, so they never build antibodies of their own; and they develop a progressive anaemia that does not respond to treatment. The combination is what kills them, and neither alone would.

The reason a foal looks well at first is maternal: it absorbs antibody from its dam's colostrum in the first day of life and lives on that borrowed immunity. As it decays over the following weeks, nothing replaces it. The foal then meets ordinary environmental organisms with no defence, and dies of an infection that would not trouble a normal foal.

Why a fatal recessive is this common
------------------------------------

It is the standard answer for a numerically small, closed, geographically isolated breed: a fatal allele that is completely silent in the heterozygote pays no penalty. Selection never sees a carrier, and in a small population drift and a few influential sires do the rest. A foundational screen found 82 carriers in 214 Fell Ponies - about 38% - and UC Davis has reported a figure near 40%.

That is also why the breed societies were able to act on it. A test has existed since 2010, and because carriers are ordinary healthy ponies the sensible management is not to cull them - in a breed this small that would be its own disaster - but to test and never pair two. The mod reproduces exactly that situation: the Fell Pony ships carrying it at the real rate, and the tool for dealing with it is information rather than avoidance.

Why the locus is named SLC5A3 and not "FIS"
Because there is a confirmed causal variant to name it after. Two loci in this mod - CVM and megaesophagus - are keyed on the condition instead, precisely because they have none, and both pages say so. This one does not need that hedge.
