Silver is a dominant dilution of black pigment caused by a mutation in PMEL (formerly PMEL17, also called SILV). It is often called silver dapple, taffy, or chocolate, but it does not affect red pigment—so chestnuts can silently carry it. [13][26][27]

Core facts
----------

- Gene: PMEL (premelanosome protein; historically PMEL17 / SILV).
- Locus / allele symbol: Z.
- Mutation: A missense variant in exon 11, Arg618Cys, is the established silver-causing variant. [13][26]
- Inheritance: Autosomal dominant. One copy produces visible silver on any horse with black-based pigment. [13][26]
- Pigment effect: Dilutes eumelanin (black/brown pigment); it has little to no effect on pheomelanin (red/yellow pigment). [11][13][27]
- Major health association: The same PMEL variant is associated with Multiple Congenital Ocular Anomalies (MCOA), with severity increasing in Z/Z horses. [12][13][21]

Genotypes
---------

Genotype | Coat expression | Breeding transmission | Eye risk
N/N | No silver dilution; cannot produce silver foals without another silver parent | 0% silver allele | No PMEL-associated MCOA risk [13]
N/Z | Silver visible on black or bay base; hidden on chestnut | 50% chance per foal | Often cyst-type/milder MCOA signs; some may be clinically subtle [13][21]
Z/Z | Silver visible on black or bay base; hidden on chestnut | 100% silver allele | Highest MCOA risk; corneal enlargement, abnormal iris/retina, vision impairment, and possible blindness can occur [13][21]

Effect on base colors
---------------------

Base phenotype | Visual effect of at least one Z allele
Black | Body diluted to chocolate, liver-brown, taupe, or silvery dark brown; mane and tail commonly flaxen to silver-gray; dappling may occur. [13][27][31]
Bay / brown | Red body remains essentially unchanged, while black mane, tail, lower legs, ear rims, and other points become silver or flaxen. [13][27][29]
Chestnut / sorrel | No visible coat change because the coat is red-pigmented; the horse can still carry and transmit Z. [13][21][27]
Buckskin, palomino, cremello-like combinations | Silver changes only the black component; its visibility depends heavily on the other dilution and base-color genes. [27]

What silver does not do
-----------------------

There is no good evidence that PMEL silver affects:

- Height or body size.
- Speed, athletic ability, or stamina.
- Muscle mass, muscle fiber type, or performance phenotype.
- Bone structure, conformation, or skeletal development.
- Hoof shape, hoof quality, or hoof pigmentation as a primary genetic effect.
- Feathering amount or texture.
- White markings, such as blaze, socks, or sabino-type patterning.

Its confirmed effects are pigmentary—black-pigment dilution—and ocular development through the MCOA association. Claims that silver changes conformation, gait, temperament, size, or performance are not supported by the established genetic literature. [11][13][21][26]

Mane and tail
-------------

Silver commonly lightens black mane and tail hair to flaxen, silver-gray, or near-white, especially on black-based horses. The effect can be less obvious on bay horses, and some horses’ manes and tails darken with age. [13][27][31]

This is distinct from the flaxen trait of chestnuts: flaxen affects red pigment in chestnut manes and tails, whereas silver affects black pigment and is invisible on a chestnut coat. [13][27]

Eye disease: MCOA
-----------------

MCOA is the most important non-cosmetic issue tied to silver. It is a pleiotropic effect: the same PMEL variant that dilutes black pigment is associated with developmental abnormalities of the eye. [12][13][21]

- N/Z horses: Often have the milder “cyst phenotype,” including translucent cysts of the iris, ciliary body, or retina; some have few or no obvious clinical signs. [21]
- Z/Z horses: At greater risk of severe abnormalities, including corneal enlargement, abnormal iris and/or retina, cataracts, glaucoma-like changes, impaired vision, and blindness. [13][21]
- Chestnut carriers: May show no silver coat but can still carry Z and produce affected offspring; DNA testing is therefore important in breeds where silver occurs. [13][21]

Established vs. speculative
---------------------------

Well established
................

- PMEL exon 11 Arg618Cys causes silver dilution. [13][26]
- Silver is dominant and affects eumelanin, not pheomelanin. [11][13][27]
- Z/Z horses have more severe MCOA than N/Z horses; the ocular effect is dosage-related. [13][21]
- Chestnuts can carry silver without expressing it visually. [13][21][27]
- Genetic testing can distinguish N/N, N/Z, and Z/Z. [13][21]

Speculative or unresolved
.........................

- Whether additional functional PMEL alleles exist in horses. The equine silver phenotype is currently well explained by the known Z variant; no widely validated second silver-dilution allele is part of standard UC Davis-style testing. [13][26]
- Whether modifier genes explain why some silver horses appear much lighter or darker than others. Body shade varies, but the precise modifiers have not been fully resolved. [25][29]
- Whether all N/Z horses develop clinically meaningful eye disease. Many have cysts, but expressivity varies; “carrier but healthy-looking” does not mean free of ocular findings. [21][22]
- Whether PMEL variation contributes to other pigmentary traits outside the recognized silver phenotype. This remains an area for further research rather than an established fact. [26]

Breeds where silver is found
----------------------------

Silver occurs across many breeds but is especially associated with:

- Rocky Mountain Horse
- Kentucky Mountain Saddle Horse
- Mountain Pleasure Horse
- Icelandic Horse
- Shetland Pony / American Shetland
- American Miniature Horse
- Morgan
- Missouri Fox Trotter
- Ardennes and Comtois draft breeds
- Gypsy Cob / Gypsy Vanner
- Welsh Pony
- Quarter Horse, Paint Horse, Appaloosa, Saddlebred, and related breeds [21][28][29][33]

The Rocky Mountain Horse and Icelandic Horse are particularly well known for silver, while Shetlands, Miniatures, and Comtois are also important populations. [22][33][34]

Practical breeder guidance
--------------------------

- Test black-based breeding stock in silver-prone breeds, especially before planning Z/Z matings. [13][21]
- Do not assume a chestnut is N/N based on appearance; chestnut masks silver. [13][21]
- Avoid deliberately breeding Z/Z to Z/Z: all foals will inherit Z, and severe MCOA risk is dosage-related. [13][21]
- Use ophthalmologic examination in addition to DNA testing, because genotype identifies risk but does not replace clinical eye assessment. [21][22]

Simulator design notes
----------------------

For a horse-breeding simulation, model silver as:

- Locus: PMEL
- Alleles: N and Z
- Dominance: Z dominant for visible black-pigment dilution
- Expression gate: Visible only if the horse has at least some eumelanin; hidden on ee chestnut
- Dosage effect: Use Z dosage for MCOA severity, not for a stronger coat dilution
- Health trait: Link Z/Z to high severe-ocular-risk and N/Z to milder cyst-type risk
- Non-traits: Do not tie silver to height, speed, muscle, bone, hoof shape, feathering, or white-marking genes.

Why it runs after agouti
------------------------

Silver only has something to do once the black points exist, so in Genes.naturalOrder() it sits immediately after agouti: agouti paints the mane / tail / leg black, silver then lightens it. Put the other way round and a bay's mane would be re-blackened after silver had already flaxened it.

Follow-ups

- The dappling now ships (2026-09-17). A BodyNoise.cellDistance lattice in body space, warped exactly as grey's is so the two kinds of dapple on one horse are the same shape of thing - but at a much shallower depth (0.16 against grey's 0.42), because a silver dapple is a shading within one chocolate rather than pigment lost. Body only; the mane and tail keep the flaxen path untouched. Its seed, spacing and strength live in the allele's own epigenetics, so they inherit with the copy and drift each breeding - two horses of the same genotype can dapple differently, and a line can be bred toward heavy dappling. Strength runs down to zero, because a silver that barely dapples is a real and common horse.
